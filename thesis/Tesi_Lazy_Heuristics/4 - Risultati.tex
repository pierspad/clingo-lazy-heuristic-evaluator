\chapter{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\section{Experimental Setup and Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

All experiments were executed on dedicated HPC cluster nodes requesting 4 cores, with hard limits of 600 seconds and 30~GB of RAM per run enforced by \texttt{runlim}. Both backends were compiled on the test hardware and the version of SWI-Prolog used was 10.0.2.

The evaluation covers the benchmark suite defined in Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
    \item BSP: $n \in \{10, 20, \ldots, 200\}$ ($20$ instances);
    \item PUP: \texttt{double-$N$}, with $N \in \{20, 40, \ldots, 200\}$ ($10$ instances);
    \item HRP: \texttt{house-$N$}, with $N \in \{2, 4, \ldots, 20\}$ persons ($10$ instances).
\end{itemize}

Across all benchmark families, timeouts represent the exclusive first failure mode (denoted \emph{T@$N$} in the summary tables), as every failing variant encounters the 600-second time limit before exhausting the memory ceiling. Memory exhaustion is confined to very large BSP instances ($n \ge 180$) where base program grounding alone exceeds the 30\,GB budget, beyond all the first failures for BSP.

On BSP, search trajectories between C++ and Prolog coincide identically across all solved instances. On PUP and HRP there are minor discrepancies in choices that arise solely from the tie-breaking among equal-rank candidates.

Evaluating these summary tables reveals that the maximum instance size solved provides different insights depending on the problem of choice. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{On BSP}, the solvability frontier alone is insufficient to differentiate the approaches. Variants sharing the same base encoding (\texttt{gc\_noheur}, \texttt{la}, and \texttt{lc}) all stop between $n=150$ and $n=160$, constrained by the heavy grounding of the base program. What distinguishes these variants is their internal search efficiency: \texttt{la} virtually eliminates solving time and conflicts (Sections~\ref{sec:bsp-runtime} and~\ref{sec:per-decision-cost}).
    \item \textbf{On PUP}, the Alpha-like dynamic guidance enables both \texttt{ga} and \texttt{la} to solve the entire range up to $N=200$, whereas the other variants stall around $N=80$ due to search space explosion. With search effectively solved, PUP isolates the cost of the heuristic representation itself: the lazy approach \texttt{la} cuts grounding time and peak memory in half compared to the static aggregate unrolling in \texttt{ga} (\textbf{12.7\,GB} vs \textbf{24.6\,GB} at $N=200$).
    \item \textbf{On HRP}, the solvability frontier is uninformative because nearly all variants solve the entire benchmark suite; the evaluation centers instead on decision quality and the severe misguidance observed in \texttt{lc}.
\end{itemize}

Some other trends emerge from the benchmarks. First, the static ground simulation (\texttt{ga}) fails prematurely on BSP at $n=70$, nine sizes earlier than the baseline, due to the combinatorial explosion of static unrolling. Second, the native C++ and Prolog backends yield identical frontiers whenever propagator queries remain cheap; where they diverge, the Prolog backend consistently fails earlier due to evaluation overhead, particularly for \texttt{lc} where the propagator is queried tens of thousands of times. Finally, \texttt{lc} illustrates two distinct failure modes caused by clingo-style aggregate evaluation over the partial assignments: on BSP, the heuristic is completely inactive because its aggregate conditions are never satisfied during search (producing zero candidates), whereas on HRP, delayed evaluation yields misleading suggestions that severely degrade search performance (both detailed in Sections~\ref{sec:bsp-semantics} and~\ref{sec:hrp}).

\section{Reduction of the Ground Heuristic Component}

The primary goal of this lazy evaluation was to reduce memory spent on materializing heuristics during grounding. This reduction can be directly observed in the number of ground heuristic objects generated as a function of instance size $n$. In standard ASP encodings, directives such as:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
#heuristic b(X) : x(X), not c(X), S = #sum{Y : c(Y)}, W = (n+1)*S+X. [W, true]
\end{minted}

incorporate the dynamic aggregate value directly into the heuristic weight. Because the atoms \texttt{c(Y)} are choice atoms whose truth values are determined only during search, the grounder cannot compute the sum ahead of time. It must therefore instantiate a separate directive for every possible $(X,S)$ pair. For $X \in \{1,\ldots,n\}$ and $S$ ranging over all reachable subset sums (i.e., integers from $0$ to $n(n+1)/2$), the total number of ground directives generated by the two symmetric rules for \texttt{b} and \texttt{c} is:
\[
2 \cdot n \cdot \Big(\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2} + 1\Big) \;=\; \Theta(n^3).
\]

In contrast, the lazy variants completely eliminate this combinatorial expansion at grounding time. The aggregate is evaluated dynamically on the current partial assignment during search, allowing the materialized heuristic representation to remain constant at just two static template facts, regardless of $n$.

\section{BSP: Runtime Behaviour}
\label{sec:bsp-runtime}

For all variants sharing the base encoding without all the grounded heuristic directives (\texttt{gc\_noheur}, \texttt{la}, and \texttt{lc}), the base program grounding alone dominates overall runtime. At $n=120$, it generates $52.8$ million rules, requiring $160.9$\,s of grounding for \texttt{gc\_noheur}, $159.7$\,s for \texttt{lc}, and $154.2$\,s for \texttt{la}.

\section{BSP: the Two Semantics Under the Microscope}
\label{sec:bsp-semantics}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bsp-search} below, \texttt{la} requires exactly $n$ decisions with zero conflicts across every benchmarked size $n$. Under Alpha-like semantics, dynamic aggregate evaluation provides the propagator with usable guidance from the very first decision on an empty assignment, guiding the CDCL solver with perfect choices.

In contrast, \texttt{lc} exhibits search statistics that are identical point-by-point to the heuristic-free baseline (\texttt{gc\_noheur}), recording $78{,}565$ choices and $49{,}993$ conflicts at $n=120$. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/bsp/native_choices.png}
    \hfill
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/bsp/native_conflicts.png}
    \caption{BSP search statistics on the C++ backend: solver choices (left) and conflicts (right) across sizes. \texttt{la} stays flat at $n$ choices with zero conflicts, while \texttt{lc} overlaps the heuristic-free baseline \texttt{gc\_noheur} point by point.}
    \label{fig:bsp-search}
\end{figure}

Under clingo semantics, aggregate guards on \texttt{c/1} require all elements to be assigned before the sum can be evaluated and an atom is considered \enquote{assigned false} only when clasp demonstrates that it cannot be true. During search, these conditions are virtually never met at decision points. At $n=120$, although the propagator is consulted on every decision, it yields zero applicable candidates across the entire run. Because both variants run on the exact same native C++ propagator backend, this comparison proves that lazy evaluation alone is insufficient if the underlying semantics cannot evaluate partial information, causing the solver to fall back entirely on default CDCL branching.

\section{BSP: Rules, Variables, and Memory}

The reduction of the heuristic footprint is also evident in the size of the propositional formula generated for the solver. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:n100-vars}, at $n=100$ the two lazy variants replace the $1{,}010{,}200$ ground directives of \texttt{gc} with the two static facts of the DSL, and their variable count ($20{,}300$) coincides exactly with the heuristic-free baseline: the heuristic leaves no trace in the propositional formula. The ground variants pay this cost in different forms. In \texttt{gc} the directives dominate the formula itself, which grows to more than one million variables, fifty times the baseline; in \texttt{ga} the variable count stays close to the baseline, but the same million directives still have to be grounded, and beyond $n=50$ this effort does not translate into usable guidance. A key result is the performance gap between execution backends: compiled C++ evaluation averages $\approx 10^{-4}$\,ms per consultation, whereas interpreted SWI-Prolog evaluation ranges from $0.2$ to $9.7$\,ms.

The overall overhead varies dramatically across the benchmark configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item When the heuristic has \textbf{perfect guidance (\texttt{la} on BSP)}, with only 120 decide calls taking $0.62$\,ms each in Prolog (and $10^{-4}$\,ms in C++), propagator overhead accounts for just $0.05\%$ of the total runtime.
    \item When the semantics gets in the way \textbf{(\texttt{lc} on BSP)}, although individual query costs remain low ($0.21$\,ms), making $78{,}565$ futile calls causes the propagator to consume $8.7\%$ of runtime ($17.7$\,s) without aiding search.
    \item When candidate generation becomes heavy \textbf{(\texttt{la} on HRP)}, producing over $1{,}200$ candidates per decision increases Prolog evaluation time to $9.68$\,ms per call; as a result, the propagator absorbs $68.3\%$ of the total runtime ($1.03$\,s out of $1.55$\,s), even though search needs only 106 decisions.
    \item When frequent search and expensive evaluation collide \textbf{(\texttt{lc} on HRP)}, making $73{,}143$ futile calls with $239$ candidates each creates a worst-case scenario: Prolog evaluation and trail synchronization eat up $99.4\%$ of the total runtime ($250$\,s out of $264.5$\,s), compared to just $1.80$\,s on the native C++ backend.
\end{itemize}

\[
\text{Total Overhead} = \text{Decide Calls} \times (\text{Query Cost} + \text{Amortized Synchronization Cost}).

Consequently, lazy evaluation is computationally cheapest precisely when the heuristic is most effective: investing additional computational effort into evaluating rich dynamic conditions per decision pays off exponentially by suppressing search space explosion.

\section{Two Ground Simulations of Alpha: \texttt{ga} versus \texttt{ga\_weak}}
\label{sec:ga-weak}

How closely can Alpha semantics be simulated within a ground-and-solve pipeline? Section~\ref{sec:ga-simulation} introduced two distinct transformations: \texttt{ga\_weak}, which simply removes negative atoms from rule bodies, and \texttt{ga}, which replaces aggregate equality checks with dynamically unrolled monotone bounds ($S \le \#\mathtt{sum}\{Y : c(Y)\}$).

In BSP, \texttt{ga} produces the exact same $\Theta(n^3)$ ground directives as the baseline \texttt{gc} ($1{,}120$ directives at $n=10$, $8{,}440$ at $n=20$, and $27{,}960$ at $n=30$). This is because keeping equality bindings rather than unrolled bounds does not reduce the grounding size: the grounder must still enumerate all subset sums. In contrast, \texttt{ga\_weak} removes the \texttt{sum\_value(S)} domain entirely, generating only $2n$ directives (Section~\ref{sec:bsp}).
The main difference is that equality bindings trigger at most once, and only after all atoms in the sum are assigned, whereas monotone bounds activate incrementally as the partial sum grows.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/bsp/expl_gaweak_solving.png}
    \hfill
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/bsp/expl_gaweak_choices.png}
    \caption{Solving time (left) and solver choices (right) for BSP comparing the Alpha-like semantics simulations (\texttt{ga} and \texttt{ga\_weak}) against the baseline variants.}
    \label{fig:gaweak}
\end{figure}

This illustrates the solving performance and search complexity of both simulation strategies. 
Beyond $n=50$, \texttt{ga} performance rapidly deteriorates as weights exceed the \texttt{int16} range and saturate at $32{,}767$: distinct partial sums collapse onto the same weight, and the ranking stops discriminating between candidate decisions. At $n=60$, it already requires $1.7$\,million choices and $137$\,s of solving time, before consistently timing out from $n=70$.

Conversely, \texttt{ga\_weak} never hits the timeout, even though its weight expression is the same as \texttt{ga}'s: the equality binding is satisfied only once all the elements of the sum are assigned, so the heuristic almost never activates and the saturation issue never arises. For this same reason, however, it provides no effective guidance during search. At $n=120$, for instance, it exhibits a degraded and noisier search trajectory, requiring $121{,}159$ choices and $69.6$\,s of solving time compared to $78{,}565$ choices and $44.1$\,s for the baseline. 

Overall, these results show that simply omitting negative guards without unrolling aggregate bounds provides no actionable heuristic guidance to the solver, increasing overall search effort and slightly reducing the solvable instance horizon.

\section{Evaluating Pre-Materialized Auxiliary Predicates}
\label{sec:aux-form}

The \texttt{la\_aux} variant investigates whether the primary advantage of lazy evaluation stems simply from reducing the number of \texttt{\_\_heuristic} directives, or from keeping the evaluation of heuristic conditions entirely outside the ground program. In \texttt{la\_aux}, heuristic conditions and aggregate values are pre-materialized into auxiliary ASP predicates (\texttt{h\_b(X,S)} and \texttt{h\_c(X,S)}), which the lazy propagator then queries during search.

Defining auxiliary rules forces the grounder to instantiate the full Cartesian product of elements and partial sums $(X, S)$. As a consequence, solver variables increase to $132{,}756$ at $n=50$ (compared to just $5{,}150$ in direct \texttt{la}), with peak RSS escalating to $27.2$\,GB at $n=170$.

Figure~\ref{fig:laaux} illustrates how this auxiliary materialization cripples performance across both backend architectures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Because the propagator must scan all materialized auxiliary candidates, the per-decision cost scales as $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$. Solving time diverges sharply from $n=40$, reaching $287.1$\,s at $n=60$ before consistently timing out from $n=70$ onward. In contrast, the direct \texttt{la} formulation maintains negligible solving times across the entire range.
    \item In the Prolog backend, the sheer volume of auxiliary candidates multiplies the required consultations, jumping from $573$ calls at $n=20$ to $8{,}452$ at $n=30$ (compared to exactly $n$ calls in direct \texttt{la}). Trail synchronization alone accounts for $99.2\%$ of the total runtime ($383$\,s out of $386$\,s) at $n=30$, causing the backend to time out for all sizes $n \ge 40$.
\end{itemize}

These findings demonstrate that merely reducing the number of heuristic directives is insufficient on its own: the benefit comes from keeping the evaluation of the heuristic conditions out of the ground program. Once those conditions are materialized as auxiliary rules, the combinatorial cost simply moves from the directives to the base encoding, and the lazy propagator inherits it at every decision.

\section{Effect of the Constraint Formulation}
\label{sec:constr-form}

The \texttt{la\_co} variant replaces the quadratic difference check over the two sums with a single constant-bound interval constraint, substantially simplifying the grounding structure.

In the standard formulations (\texttt{gc}, \texttt{la}, and \texttt{lc}), this quadratic constraint dominates the grounding phase: at $n=120$, it produces approximately $52.8$\,million propositional rules and requires between one and three minutes of grounding time. While direct lazy evaluation (\texttt{la}) successfully eliminates heuristic directive bloat, the ground program remains constrained by the sheer size of the domain specification.

\section{PUP: the Grounding Bottleneck at Scale}
\label{sec:pup}

The Partner Units Problem (PUP) represents an industrial configuration benchmark where domain-specific heuristics effectively tame combinatorial search. Unlike BSP (which relies exclusively on \texttt{\#sum}), PUP combines dynamic \texttt{\#count} and \texttt{\#max} aggregates---notably using \texttt{\#max} to track remaining capacity across adjacent partner units.

In BSP, unguided search dominated baseline execution times; in PUP, activating the dynamic Alpha-like semantics reduces solving effort to just a few hundred decisions across all instance sizes. Consequently, the solving phase becomes almost trivial, shifting the performance bottleneck almost entirely ($>95\%$ of total execution time) to the grounding phase and memory consumption. This makes PUP the ideal benchmark to evaluate the grounding and memory efficiency of lazy evaluation at scale.

\section{An External Reference: Alpha}
\label{sec:alpha}

To evaluate how closely our propagator reproduces the lazy evaluation process and performance advantages, we compare our clingo variants against Alpha with and without its declarative heuristic engine (Qh mode). 

On BSP, the guided Alpha configuration (\texttt{alpha\_qh}) solves all twenty instances in $3.4$\,s and $0.37$\,GB at $n=200$, requiring exactly $n$ decisions and zero backtracks. This matches the search trajectory achieved by \texttt{la} in clingo ($n$ choices, zero conflicts). The performance gap at larger sizes ($585.7$\,s for \texttt{la} at $n=160$ versus $2.4$\,s for \texttt{alpha\_qh}) stems entirely from base program grounding in gringo ($52.8$ million rules at $n=120$), which Alpha completely avoids by instantiating rules lazily along the search trail.

\begin{itemize}

\section{C++ versus Prolog: Backend Comparison}
\label{sec:backend-summary}

Across the benchmark campaign, the compiled C++ and interpreted Prolog backends demonstrate consistent behavior, producing identical solvability frontiers in all but five specific configurations. Whenever heuristic consultations are infrequent or computationally lightweight---such as in \texttt{la} on PUP and HRP---both engines achieve the exact same problem reach.

Where the two backends diverge, the Prolog engine consistently fails earlier due to evaluation and synchronization overhead. On BSP, the frontier drops by a single step for \texttt{la} ($n=160 \to 150$) and \texttt{lc} ($150 \to 140$) because both runs finish within seconds of the 600-second time limit. On PUP ($N=100 \to 40$) and HRP ($N=16 \to 14$) under \texttt{lc}, the unguided search triggers tens of thousands of futile queries, turning Prolog's per-call interpretation cost into the dominant bottleneck.

The C++ backend evaluates heuristic decisions three to four orders of magnitude faster than the interpreted Prolog engine ($\approx 10^{-4}$\,ms versus $0.2$--$9.7$\,ms per call); hence, the native backend provides maximum raw performance for solving, while the embedded Prolog backend offers expressive relational querying and transparent rapid prototyping.

\section{Summary of Results}
\label{sec:results-summary}

The empirical evaluation demonstrates that lazy heuristic evaluation fundamentally changes how domain-specific guidance interacts with ground-and-solve ASP. By shifting dynamic aggregate evaluation from grounding to search, the framework reduces the heuristic representation from a combinatorial $\Theta(n^3)$ explosion down to two constant template facts on BSP. In realistic configuration benchmarks like PUP, this translates into halving both grounding time ($225.8$\,s versus $436.6$\,s) and peak memory ($12.7$\,GB versus $24.6$\,GB) at scale compared to static ground simulation (\texttt{ga}). Furthermore, the exploratory variants confirm that this benefit requires keeping the evaluation entirely dynamic: omitting negative guards without unrolling dynamic aggregates (\texttt{ga\_weak}) fails to provide actionable search guidance, while pre-materializing heuristic bodies into auxiliary ASP rules (\texttt{la\_aux}) reintroduces the combinatorial explosion into the base program. Conversely, pairing lazy heuristics with compact domain constraints (\texttt{la\_co}) eliminates grounding and solving bottlenecks simultaneously, resolving BSP up to $n=200$ in milliseconds.

The campaign also proves that evaluation semantics is decisive for search guidance. Under Alpha-like semantics, dynamic aggregate evaluation provides immediate, actionable advice on partial assignments, achieving near-perfect search on BSP ($n$ choices with zero conflicts) and faithful multi-level ranking on HRP. In contrast, standard clingo semantics requires complete assignments to evaluate aggregate bodies, rendering the heuristic completely inert on BSP and actively misleading on HRP, where delayed high-level rules allow incomplete lower-level rules to steer the solver into massive search subspaces. Moreover, while static ground unrolling (\texttt{ga}) can approximate dynamic aggregates on small instances, it slows down grounding and breaks down whenever the flattened weights overflow clasp's 16-bit ceiling ($32{,}767$), a limitation that the lazy propagator avoids entirely by sorting decisions internally.

Finally, lazy evaluation trades grounding memory for decision-time computation, where total overhead factorizes into decision frequency multiplied by per-consultation cost. As a result, the lazy mechanism is computationally cheapest precisely when the heuristic guides well, as the solver makes only a minimal number of decisions. Compared against the reference lazy solver Alpha, our clingo propagator reproduces the exact reference search trajectories, coming within a factor of $4.6\times$ in execution time and $1.2\times$ in peak memory on PUP while avoiding the static unrolling overhead of standard ground-and-solve systems.
