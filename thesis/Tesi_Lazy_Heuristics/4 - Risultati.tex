\chapter{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\section{Experimental Setup and Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

All experiments were executed on dedicated HPC cluster nodes requesting 4 cores, with hard limits of 600 seconds and 30~GB of RAM per run enforced by \texttt{runlim}. Both backends were compiled on the test hardware and the version of SWI-Prolog used was 10.0.2.

The chapter first examines heuristic representation and search behaviour on BSP, PUP and HRP, using the native backend for the family plots. Section~\ref{sec:backend-summary} then compares C++ and Prolog directly, and Section~\ref{sec:per-decision-cost} explains their runtime costs. The remaining sections compare against Alpha, analyze the clingo-semantics variants, and test the ground simulations, auxiliary predicates and compact BSP encoding.

The evaluation covers the benchmark suite defined in Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
    \item BSP: $n \in \{10, 20, \ldots, 200\}$ ($20$ instances);
    \item PUP: \texttt{double-$N$}, with $N \in \{20, 40, \ldots, 200\}$ ($10$ instances);
    \item HRP: \texttt{house-$N$}, with $N \in \{2, 4, \ldots, 20\}$ persons ($10$ instances).
\end{itemize}

Across all benchmark families, every variant that fails at all fails first by timeout (denoted \emph{T@$N$} in the summary tables): the 600-second limit is always reached before the 30\,GB ceiling, so the frontiers reported here are time-bound throughout. Memory exhaustion does occur, but only on the standard BSP encoding at $n = 180$, where grounding the base program alone exceeds the budget; that is beyond the first failure point of every variant sharing that encoding and therefore never determines one.

On BSP the two backends have identical choice and conflict counts on every instance both solve. On PUP and HRP, \texttt{la} choice counts differ by up to about fourteen percent relative to C++; differences can be larger for \texttt{lc}. Section~\ref{sec:backend-summary} discusses the effects of candidate ordering and auxiliary predicates on this comparison.

\subsection{Campaign Overview and Failure Modes}
\label{sec:campaign-overview}

Tables~\ref{tab:overview-bsp}--\ref{tab:overview-hrp} summarize the outcome of the benchmark campaign, reporting the number of solved instances and the first failure point for each variant.
In the BSP benchmark, solver failures scale predictably with instance size: once a variant fails at a given size, it also fails for all subsequent sizes, with timeouts representing the first failure mode in all cases (between $n=70$ and $n=170$). On PUP, however, difficulty does not scale monotonically with size: \texttt{lc} times out on \texttt{double-80} yet successfully solves \texttt{double-100}. With one instance and one run per size, the campaign cannot separate instance-specific difficulty from run-to-run variability. Section~\ref{sec:pup} examines the associated search effort.

The maximum instance size solved provides different insights depending on the problem of choice.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{On BSP}, the solvability frontier alone does not separate the approaches. Variants sharing the same base encoding (\texttt{gc\_noheur}, \texttt{la}, and \texttt{lc}) all stop between $n=150$ and $n=160$, constrained by the heavy grounding of the base program. What distinguishes them is search efficiency: \texttt{la} reduces solving time and conflicts to near zero (Sections~\ref{sec:bsp-runtime} and~\ref{sec:per-decision-cost}).
    \item \textbf{On PUP} (Table~\ref{tab:overview-pup}), the Alpha-like dynamic guidance lets both \texttt{ga} and \texttt{la} solve the entire range up to $N=200$, whereas the other variants stall around $N=80$ under search space explosion. With search no longer the bottleneck, PUP isolates the cost of the heuristic representation itself: \texttt{la} halves both grounding time and peak memory with respect to the static aggregate unrolling of \texttt{ga} (\textbf{12.7\,GB} vs \textbf{24.4\,GB} at $N=200$).
    \item \textbf{On HRP}, the frontier is uninformative because nearly all variants solve the entire suite; the evaluation centers instead on decision quality and on the misguidance observed in \texttt{lc}.
\end{itemize}

Three further trends are visible in the tables. First, the static ground simulation (\texttt{ga}) fails on BSP already at $n=70$, nine sizes earlier than the baseline, because saturation of clasp's weight field disrupts the heuristic ranking (Section~\ref{sec:ga-weak}). Second, the native C++ and Prolog backends yield identical frontiers whenever propagator queries remain cheap; where they diverge, the Prolog backend usually fails earlier due to evaluation overhead, particularly for \texttt{lc}, where the propagator is queried tens of thousands of times. The one exception is the \texttt{la\_aux} ablation, which reaches two sizes \emph{further} on Prolog, for the reason analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:per-decision-cost}. Third, \texttt{lc} shows two distinct failure modes of the clingo-like reading over partial assignments: on BSP the heuristic never activates, because its aggregate conditions are not satisfied at any decision point, whereas on HRP delayed evaluation produces misleading suggestions that degrade search by orders of magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:clingo-semantics}).


\section{Reduction of the Ground Heuristic Component}
\label{sec:footprint}

The primary benefit of lazy evaluation is eliminating the materialization of heuristic directives during grounding. Figure~\ref{fig:combined-heuristics} and Table~\ref{tab:heur-growth} compare the number of heuristic entries the grounder materializes across instance sizes on BSP between the ground variants (\texttt{gc}, \texttt{ga}, \texttt{ga\_weak}) and the lazy ones (\texttt{la} and \texttt{lc}, together with the exploratory \texttt{la\_aux} and \texttt{la\_co}).

As established in Section~\ref{sec:bsp}, static aggregate unrolling incurs a theoretical $\Theta(n^3)$ combinatorial explosion. The empirical counts reflect this scaling directly: the ground variants scale rapidly from $1{,}120$ directives at $n=10$ to over $1.34$ million at $n=110$. In contrast, the lazy representation remains invariant at exactly two template facts across all sizes, yielding a reduction factor of over $670{,}000\times$ at $n=110$.

\section{BSP: Runtime Behaviour}
\label{sec:bsp-runtime}

For all variants sharing the base encoding without grounded heuristic directives (\texttt{gc\_noheur}, \texttt{la}, and \texttt{lc}), grounding dominates overall execution time. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bsp-grounding}, their grounding curves remain close across the benchmark range, as none of them materializes heuristic rules during grounding.

The advantage of dynamic evaluation emerges in search performance (Figure~\ref{fig:bsp-solving}): while \texttt{gc\_noheur} and \texttt{lc} require $44.8$\,s and $49.1$\,s of solving at $n=120$, \texttt{la} completes the search in just $0.04$\,s and remains near zero across all instance sizes.

Although \texttt{la} achieves the lowest total runtime among the three variants (Figure~\ref{fig:bsp-total}), the overall improvement is approximately $25\%$. Dynamic evaluation under the Alpha-like reading therefore renders search practically negligible, but overall scaling on standard BSP remains governed by the grounding of the base program, a limitation examined further below.

The search statistics in Table~\ref{tab:bsp-n120} and Figure~\ref{fig:bsp-search} highlight three key observations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item In \texttt{la}, default negation and dynamic aggregate evaluation let clingo use the heuristic from the first decision, taking exactly 120 choices with zero conflicts at $n=120$. Figure~\ref{fig:bsp-search} shows that this is not a property of one size: \texttt{la} requires exactly $n$ decisions with zero conflicts at every benchmarked $n$. Dynamic aggregate evaluation gives the propagator usable guidance from the first decision on an empty assignment, and every choice it proposes lies on the solution path.
    \item \texttt{ga} matches \texttt{la} exactly up to $n=50$, then degrades at $n=60$ and times out from $n=70$ onward. The cause is clasp's 16-bit weight field, as explained in Section~\ref{sec:weight-problem}. Section~\ref{sec:ga-weak} analyzes the two ground simulations in detail.
    \item The frontier itself, however, is not moved by any of this. \texttt{gc\_noheur}, \texttt{lc} and \texttt{la} stop within one size of each other, because around $n = 160$ grounding the base program, already $52.8$ million rules at $n=120$, leaves little or no time for search. On BSP the claim is therefore about cost and not about reach. Section~\ref{sec:pup} exhibits the family where it is about reach; Section~\ref{sec:constr-form} what happens when the base program is made compact as well; and Section~\ref{sec:co-family} what becomes of the weight-field limit just described, which turns out to bind the ranking rather than the encoding.
\end{enumerate}

\section{BSP: Rules, Variables, and Memory}
\label{sec:bsp-memory}

The reduction of the heuristic footprint is also evident in the size of the propositional formula generated for the solver. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:n100-vars}, at $n=100$ the two lazy variants replace the $1{,}010{,}200$ ground directives of \texttt{gc} with the two template facts of the DSL, and their variable count ($20{,}300$) coincides exactly with the heuristic-free baseline: the heuristic leaves no trace in the propositional formula. The ground variants pay this cost in different forms. In \texttt{gc} the directives dominate the formula itself, which grows to more than one million variables, fifty times the baseline; in \texttt{ga} the variable count stays close to the baseline, but the same million directives still have to be grounded, and beyond $n=50$ this effort does not translate into usable guidance. Only \texttt{la\_co} falls below the baseline, with $101$ variables, because it also reformulates the balance constraint (Section~\ref{sec:constr-form}).

\section{PUP: the Grounding Bottleneck at Scale}
\label{sec:pup}

Unlike BSP, which uses only \texttt{\#sum}, PUP combines dynamic \texttt{\#count} and \texttt{\#max} aggregates, the latter tracking the remaining capacity across adjacent partner units.

In standard BSP grounding dominates baseline execution time, and the base program owns the frontier for every variant that shares it. In PUP the dynamic Alpha-like semantics reduces solving effort to a few hundred decisions at every instance size, so more than $95\%$ of the total execution time moves to grounding and the cost becomes one of memory. With search largely removed from the picture, the dominant separation between the variants is the size of the heuristic representation they had to build, which is the quantity Section~\ref{sec:footprint} measures in the abstract, here measured in seconds and gigabytes.

Figure~\ref{fig:pup-total} and Table~\ref{tab:pup-frontier} summarize the overall performance across all five variants under a $600$\,s timeout and a $30$\,GB memory limit. The family plots connect successful measurements; the \texttt{lc} segment from $N=60$ to $N=100$ spans the timeout at $N=80$.

\paragraph{The unguided baseline.}
Without dynamic guidance the solver relies entirely on VSIDS, and combinatorial choices escalate rapidly: \texttt{gc\_noheur} takes $546{,}549$ choices at $N=80$ and times out at $N=100$, where it reaches $3.38$\,million. The two clingo-semantics variants \texttt{gc} and \texttt{lc} fail in the same region; Section~\ref{sec:clingo-semantics} analyzes them together with their BSP and HRP counterparts.

\paragraph{Grounding and memory savings (\texttt{la} vs \texttt{ga}).}
Both Alpha-like semantics variants (\texttt{ga} and \texttt{la}) solve the entire benchmark horizon up to $N=200$, maintaining near-perfect search guidance ($235$ choices for \texttt{ga} vs $162$ for \texttt{la} at $N=120$). With respect to \texttt{la}, \texttt{ga} has dropped the negative guards and reconstructed the \texttt{\#max} capacity terms through unrolled bounds (Section~\ref{sec:ga-simulation}), and its zone directives, whose flattened weights start at $100{,}000$, all saturate clasp's $16$-bit field (Section~\ref{sec:weight-problem}). That last effect does not invert the two priority levels, since the largest sensor weight on these instances is $26{,}220$ and stays under the ceiling, so zones are still decided before sensors; what it costs \texttt{ga} is the ranking \emph{within} the zone level, which \texttt{la} keeps because it ranks its targets internally. Because solving takes only a few seconds (Figure~\ref{fig:pup-solving}), the overall performance is nonetheless differentiated almost exclusively by the grounding footprint.

The saving holds across the whole range: the ratio between the two variants is largest on the small instances and settles just below a factor of two at $N=200$, in both time and memory.

\section{HRP: Guidance Without Dynamic Aggregates}
\label{sec:hrp}

HRP evaluates heuristic semantics on rules with nested default negations but without dynamic aggregates. It therefore does not test the representation mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:footprint} at all: there is no aggregate domain to unroll, so the ground and the lazy forms of the heuristic are comparable in size. Because HRP instances are relatively small, nearly all variants solve every instance in under a second on the C++ backend, so the family measures guidance quality (Figure~\ref{fig:hrp-choices}) and per-decision overhead (Figure~\ref{fig:backend-semantics}) rather than reach.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/hrp/native_choices.png}
    \caption{HRP: solver choices on the C++ backend. The only diverging curve is \texttt{lc}, analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:clingo-semantics}.}
    \label{fig:hrp-choices}
\end{figure}

Under the Alpha-like reading, \texttt{la} and \texttt{ga} land within a few choices of each other at every size ($86$ vs $88$ at $N=14$, $122$ vs $116$ at $N=20$, with zero conflicts throughout, Figure~\ref{fig:hrp-choices}). These counts are consistent with the intended ranking policy, but do not establish semantic equivalence. On this family \texttt{ga} is not a reference but an approximation, since its ground form drops the capacity guards \texttt{not fullCabinet(C)} and \texttt{not assignedThing(T)} rather than reinterpreting them (Section~\ref{sec:ga-simulation}); \texttt{la} evaluates those guards on the trail. Section~\ref{sec:alpha} compares these results against Alpha itself.

HRP is also the family on which the Prolog backend is most expensive per decision, because the multi-room placement rules join multiple domain predicates and the engine must enumerate over a thousand candidate pairs at every call (Figure~\ref{fig:backend-semantics}). That cost belongs to the cost model and is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:per-decision-cost}.

\section{The Price of Laziness: Per-Decision Cost}
\label{sec:per-decision-cost}

The lazy evaluation model represents a space--time trade-off: instead of a combinatorial cost in memory during the grounding phase, there is a cost in computation incurred during search, through state updates and decision callbacks. Now that all three families have been presented, that cost can be reported for all of them at once. There is also a remarkable difference between the two backends: the C++ one is orders of magnitude faster in its decisions.

The overall overhead varies dramatically across the benchmark configurations, and Table~\ref{tab:per-decision} is ordered to make the four regimes visible:
\begin{itemize}
    \item When the heuristic has \textbf{perfect guidance (\texttt{la} on BSP)}, with only 120 decide calls taking $0.45$\,ms each in Prolog (and $10^{-4}$\,ms in C++), propagator overhead accounts for less than $0.05\%$ of the total runtime.
    \item When the semantics gets in the way \textbf{(\texttt{lc} on BSP)}, each single query is very cheap ($0.07$\,ms) because it finds nothing to return, but the calls are made $78{,}565$ times instead of $120$: the propagator ends up costing $2.6\%$ of the total runtime, roughly eighty times what it costs in \texttt{la}, and none of that work helps the search.
    \item When candidate generation becomes heavy \textbf{(\texttt{la} on HRP)}, producing over $1{,}200$ candidates per decision, Prolog evaluation time rises to $71.9$\,ms per call; as a result, the propagator absorbs $94\%$ of the total runtime ($7.6$\,s out of $8.1$\,s, against $0.41$\,s of total clingo runtime on the compiled backend), even though search needs only about 100 decisions.
    \item When frequent search and expensive evaluation collide \textbf{(\texttt{lc} on HRP)}, the two factors multiply until the run no longer completes: a run that the native backend clears with $66{,}717$ choices in $1.80$\,s exhausts the $600$\,s budget on the Prolog one. \end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:bsp-instrumentation} separates the two components that determine the Prolog backend's runtime: the number of decision calls and the evaluation cost per call. The lower plot isolates the inference time spent inside the Prolog query itself, which constitutes the vast majority of the per-decision cost (detailed in Table~\ref{tab:per-decision}). At $n=120$, for instance, the Prolog query accounts for $0.37$\,ms out of the total $0.45$\,ms per decision in \texttt{la}, and $0.043$\,ms out of $0.067$\,ms in \texttt{lc}, with the remainder spent on candidate matching and literal lookups on the C++ side. 

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Under Alpha semantics (\texttt{la}):} The query actively identifies all eligible candidates on the partial trail, performing term unification and dynamic weight computation. Although this makes each individual decision more expensive ($0.37$\,ms), the guidance is accurate, keeping the total number of decision calls to a minimum.
    \item \textbf{Under clingo semantics (\texttt{lc}):} Negative body literals fail immediately on partial assignments, so the query aborts after finding zero candidates. While this allows each call to terminate roughly eight times faster ($0.043$\,ms), it provides no guidance whatsoever to the solver, triggering a combinatorial explosion in the number of decision calls (upper plot).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where the Prolog cost actually sits.}
The profiling data above reflects an architectural redesign of the Prolog backend. In our initial implementation, state synchronization between clingo and Prolog was a severe bottleneck. Every change on the solver trail was mirrored into Prolog's dynamic database by formatting the assignment as a text string and parsing it with \texttt{PL\_chars\_to\_term}. This serialization overhead dominated execution: on the PUP benchmark at $N=200$ (\texttt{double-200}), trail synchronization consumed $17.5$\,s, compared to just $1.75$\,s of actual query evaluation.

To eliminate this bottleneck, the current design avoids mirroring the trail into Prolog altogether (Section~\ref{sec:decision-prolog}). Instead, the solver exposes its assignment directly to Prolog through non-deterministic \emph{foreign predicates} (\texttt{true\_atom/1} and \texttt{false\_atom/1}). Recording a trail assignment is therefore reduced to a native C++ hash-map update, with zero Prolog overhead.

The impact of this redesign is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pup-state-sync} and Table~\ref{tab:per-decision}. On \texttt{double-200}, synchronization time drops from $17.5$\,s to $0.63$\,s, a $27\times$ speedup that brings it on par with the native C++ backend ($0.68$\,s). As a result, the primary computational cost shifts from state synchronization to actual heuristic inference ($3.25$\,s of query time versus $0.63$\,s of synchronization).

However, accessing the trail via foreign predicates introduces a new trade-off that depends on argument binding:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Ground arguments (lookup):} When arguments are known constants (as in the membership tests of \texttt{alpha\_not/1} and \texttt{target\_available/1}), the predicate performs an immediate $O(1)$ hash-map lookup on the C++ side.
    \item \textbf{Non-ground arguments (generation):} When an argument contains unbound variables, the predicate must act as a generator. In the current implementation, it does so by copying all matching atoms into a temporary vector before iterating over them.
\end{itemize}
Because of this vector copy, heuristic rules that perform heavy relational joins and candidate enumeration incur an overhead that the previous dynamic-database design avoided, since Prolog could index clauses directly in its internal database without intermediate copying.

The HRP benchmark clearly illustrates this drawback. Its placement rules join multiple domain predicates, producing $1{,}248$ candidate bindings per decision. Under the new foreign-predicate design, the per-decision evaluation cost rises from $9.19$\,ms to $71.9$\,ms. This slowdown turns \texttt{house-20} from a $1.5$\,s run into an $8.1$\,s one, and pushes \texttt{lc} on \texttt{house-14} past the time limit.

In summary, the transition to foreign predicates is a decisive win wherever state synchronization dominated (such as in PUP, where the \texttt{la\_aux} variant of Section~\ref{sec:aux-form} solves five additional instances). However, it introduces a performance penalty on encodings that require heavy candidate enumeration. Section~\ref{sec:future-work} outlines how direct indexed iteration without vector copying can resolve this remaining limitation.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/pup/prolog_state_sync.png}
    \caption{Cumulative state synchronization time for the Prolog backend on PUP, with \texttt{true\_atom/1} and \texttt{false\_atom/1} as foreign predicates. For \texttt{la} it grows smoothly to $0.63$\,s at $N=200$, below the $3.25$\,s the same run spends inside the Prolog query; the \texttt{lc} curve, which pays one update per literal on an unguided search, reaches $6$\,s already at $N=40$ and stops there.}
    \label{fig:pup-state-sync}
\end{figure}

The PUP comparison in Figure~\ref{fig:backend-semantics} shows that solving time for \texttt{la} on PUP stays under ten seconds across the whole horizon on both backends, with the Prolog engine holding a small and stable factor over the native one. Both backends deliver guidance of broadly similar quality here, keeping the search down to a few hundred choices on either engine.



The total lazy overhead therefore factorizes as:
\[
\text{Total Overhead} = \text{Decide Calls} \times (\text{Query Cost} + \text{Amortized Synchronization Cost}),
\]
where Query Cost includes the decision callback and candidate handling, and synchronization is amortized over the decision calls. Figure~\ref{fig:backend-semantics} reports overall solving time, which also includes clasp's own search work.
Effective guidance reduces the number of consultations: a richer condition costs more per decision, but a heuristic that guides well leaves few decisions to pay for.

\section{An External Reference: Alpha}
\label{sec:alpha}

To evaluate how closely our propagator reproduces the lazy evaluation process and performance advantages, we compare our clingo variants against Alpha with and without its declarative heuristic engine (Qh mode), on all three benchmark families. BSP and PUP carry the dynamic aggregates the mechanism is designed for and are treated first; HRP, whose reference heuristic has none, is covered separately below because it answers a different question.

On BSP, the guided Alpha configuration (\texttt{alpha\_qh}) solves all twenty instances in $4.2$\,s and $0.38$\,GB at $n=200$, requiring exactly $n$ decisions and zero backtracks. The \texttt{la} variant in clingo likewise takes $n$ choices with zero conflicts, although the counters alone do not establish identical decision sequences. The performance gap at larger sizes ($579.7$\,s for \texttt{la} at $n=160$ versus $2.3$\,s for \texttt{alpha\_qh}, both on the \texttt{runlim} clock) is dominated by base program grounding in gringo ($52.8$ million rules at $n=120$), whereas Alpha avoids materialising the full ground program before search by instantiating rules lazily along the search trail.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/alpha/bsp_time.png}\\[6pt]
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/alpha/bsp_mem.png}
    \caption{BSP benchmark dashboard: the four clingo variants and the heuristic-free baseline \texttt{gc\_noheur} compared against Alpha with (\texttt{alpha\_qh}) and without (\texttt{alpha\_noheur}) declarative heuristics. Top: total wall-clock time; bottom: peak RSS. Both are \texttt{runlim} external process measures.}
    \label{fig:alpha-bsp}
\end{figure}

Lazy grounding alone is not sufficient without dynamic heuristic guidance: as Figure~\ref{fig:alpha-bsp} shows, \texttt{alpha\_noheur} solves nothing beyond $n=40$, eleven sizes short of clingo's ground baseline \texttt{gc\_noheur} ($n=150$). The two systems sit on opposite sides of one trade-off:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{clingo:} Because the program is fully grounded before search begins, exploring unpromising search branches is computationally very cheap: clasp only has to perform fast boolean propagation and VSIDS variable scoring over pre-existing clauses in compiled C++.
    \item \textbf{Alpha:} Because rules are grounded on demand during search, exploring unpromising branches can require additional rule instantiation and nogood generation.
\end{itemize}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:alpha-internals}, \texttt{alpha\_noheur} suffers a severe search space explosion, peaking at over $67{,}000$ guesses and backtracks at $n=30$; it still solves $n=40$, where each guess costs more even though fewer are needed, and from $n=50$ it no longer finishes at all. With heuristic guidance, \texttt{alpha\_qh} never backtracks on these instances and grounds rules along a successful search path. On this benchmark, dynamic guidance and lazy grounding therefore complement each other.

\section{The clingo Reading of Partial Information: \texttt{gc} and \texttt{lc}}
\label{sec:clingo-semantics}

The two variants on the clingo side of the semantics axis do not help on any benchmark of this suite, and they fail in ways that are worth separating. This section collects them, because reporting the same negative result three times inside three different family sections obscures both the result and the families.

The outcome should not be read as a defect of clingo's semantics. The heuristics of this suite were written for Alpha, whose reading of \texttt{not a} and of an aggregate over choice atoms is the one the encodings assume; \texttt{gc} and \texttt{lc} evaluate those same bodies under a semantics that requires the information to be settled before it may be read. What the campaign measures is therefore the cost of the mismatch, and the reason the semantics axis of Section~\ref{sec:experimental-design} has to exist as an axis: it is not a free parameter, and picking the wrong value for a given encoding costs everything the heuristic was supposed to buy. The three families exhibit the two possible outcomes of that mismatch.

\subsection{BSP: inertness}

On BSP, \texttt{lc} exhibits search statistics that are identical point-by-point to the heuristic-free baseline \texttt{gc\_noheur}, recording $78{,}565$ choices and $49{,}993$ conflicts at $n=120$ (Table~\ref{tab:bsp-n120} and Figure~\ref{fig:bsp-search}). Under clingo semantics, aggregate guards on \texttt{c/1} require all elements to be assigned before the sum can be evaluated, and an atom counts as \enquote{assigned false} only once clasp has shown it cannot be true. During search these conditions are never met at a decision point: at $n=120$ the propagator is consulted on every decision and yields zero applicable candidates across the entire run.

Because \texttt{la} and \texttt{lc} run on the same native C++ backend and on the same ground problem rules, apart from the semantics annotation, the comparison isolates the semantics exactly. What it establishes is a statement about this encoding, not about the approach: \emph{when} a heuristic body is written so that its guards can only be settled by a complete assignment, moving its evaluation into search buys nothing, because there is no partial information the clingo reading is willing to look at. The BSP heuristic is the extreme case of that shape, since its aggregate ranges over the very choice atoms the heuristic is meant to decide, and its negative guard tests the target's own complement.

The ground twin \texttt{gc} does worse than inert. It carries the same never-firing conditions, but materializes them: the $1{,}010{,}200$ directives at $n=100$ enter the propositional formula and multiply the variable count by fifty (Table~\ref{tab:n100-vars}). At $n=120$ this costs $296.6$\,s of solving against the baseline's $44.8$\,s, and the frontier drops from $n=150$ to $n=130$. A heuristic that never fires is not neutral if the solver has to carry its ground image.

\subsection{PUP: the same inertness, with the same surcharge}

PUP repeats the pattern on a different aggregate. \texttt{la} and \texttt{lc} produce the same ground problem rules ($216{,}193$ rules at $N=20$), differing exclusively in the semantics selector inside the propagator, so this is again a controlled comparison of the reading alone:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{la} evaluates dynamic aggregates over currently true atoms, solving $N=120$ in $48.2$\,s with only $162$ choices.
    \item \texttt{lc} waits for complete assignments, generating zero active candidates during search. At $N=120$, it makes $49{,}261$ choices and times out.
\end{itemize}
The unguided \texttt{lc} runs also exhibit a non-monotonic failure pattern in Table~\ref{tab:pup-frontier} (timing out on \texttt{double-80} but solving \texttt{double-100}). This non-monotonic outcome shows that instance size alone does not determine search difficulty. With one run per instance, the campaign cannot distinguish instance-specific effects from run-to-run variability or establish the role of restarts.

Here too the ground variant is the worse of the two, and for the same reason: \texttt{gc} fails at $N=80$ with $874{,}565$ choices, \emph{earlier} than the heuristic-free baseline, which solves that same size in $546{,}549$ choices and only fails at $N=100$. The inert ground directives add variables to the solver, enlarge the search space, and degrade VSIDS branching. On both BSP and PUP, therefore, the standard ground heuristic under the standard semantics is measurably worse than writing no heuristic at all.

\subsection{HRP: misguidance}

HRP produces the opposite failure, and it is the more instructive of the two. The HRP heuristic has no dynamic aggregates and is organized in five priority levels; under the clingo reading, the negation in the higher-level rules delays them, while the lower-level rules, whose bodies carry no such guard, fire on incomplete information. The heuristic therefore does \emph{not} go quiet: it actively steers, in the wrong order.

The cost is substantial. \texttt{lc} requires $66{,}717$ choices at $N=14$ against $86$ for \texttt{la}, an increase of nearly eight hundred times; at $N=16$ it takes $14.2$ million choices, five orders of magnitude; from $N=18$ it no longer finishes (Figure~\ref{fig:hrp-choices}). Both backends show deteriorating search under \texttt{lc}, but Prolog reaches the time limit before exploring a comparable number of decisions on the larger instances.

On the Prolog backend this is one of the configurations that the compiled backend solves and the interpreted one does not: misdirected search and relational query cost compound until \texttt{house-14}, which the native backend clears in $1.80$\,s, exhausts the whole $600$\,s budget. The Prolog frontier on this family is therefore two sizes short of the native one, $N=12$ against $N=16$, and Section~\ref{sec:per-decision-cost} locates the cost precisely.

\subsection{What the three cases share}

For the lazy clingo-like variant in this suite, the mode of failure reflects the shape of the heuristic body:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
    \item if every guard needs a complete assignment, the heuristic is \emph{inert} (BSP, PUP). Exact agreement with baseline search counters is observed on BSP, but not on PUP;
    \item if some guards need a complete assignment and others do not, the surviving portion of the heuristic fires out of order and the heuristic becomes \emph{misleading}, which is far worse than inert (HRP);
    \item on BSP and PUP, materializing ineffective directives adds grounding cost; this does not extend to HRP, where \texttt{gc} solves the entire suite and \texttt{lc} does not.
\end{itemize}
These observations distinguish the applicability of a heuristic from the cost of representing and evaluating it. An inactive lazy heuristic still incurs callback overhead, and inactivity alone does not guarantee the same search trajectory as a separately grounded baseline.

\section{Two Ground Simulations of Alpha: \texttt{ga} versus \texttt{ga\_weak}}
\label{sec:ga-weak}

How closely can Alpha semantics be simulated within a ground-and-solve pipeline? Section~\ref{sec:ga-simulation} introduced two distinct transformations: \texttt{ga\_weak}, which removes negative atoms from rule bodies, and \texttt{ga}, which replaces aggregate equality checks with statically unrolled monotone bounds ($S \le \#\mathtt{sum}\{Y : c(Y)\}$).

Neither transformation is a general-purpose rewriting, and the results below are to be read within the condition established in Section~\ref{sec:ga-simulation}: a sufficient condition for deleting a negative literal while preserving the Alpha reading is that the deleted atom is the target or its complement. BSP satisfies that condition, so the negation transformation is faithful on this family. Only \texttt{ga} also reconstructs the dynamic aggregate; \texttt{ga\_weak} is an ablation, and \texttt{ga} remains subject to weight saturation. HRP does not, which is why the corresponding HRP discussion (Section~\ref{sec:hrp}) treats \texttt{ga} as an approximation rather than as a reference.

In BSP, both \texttt{ga} and \texttt{ga\_weak} produce exactly the same $\Theta(n^3)$ ground directives as \texttt{gc} ($1{,}120$ directives at $n=10$, $8{,}440$ at $n=20$, and $27{,}960$ at $n=30$). Dropping the \texttt{sum\_value(S)} domain in \texttt{ga\_weak} does not reduce the grounding size: with the equality binding in place, the grounder must still enumerate all subset sums.
Along a search branch, the equality binding becomes available only after all atoms in the sum are assigned, whereas monotone bounds activate incrementally as the partial sum grows. Backtracking can undo either condition.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/bsp/expl_gaweak_solving.png}\\[6pt]
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/bsp/expl_gaweak_choices.png}
    \caption{Solving time (top) and solver choices (bottom) for BSP comparing the Alpha-like semantics simulations (\texttt{ga} and \texttt{ga\_weak}) against the baseline variants.}
    \label{fig:gaweak}
\end{figure}

Beyond $n=50$, \texttt{ga} deteriorates rapidly as weights exceed the \texttt{int16} range and saturate at $32{,}767$: distinct partial sums collapse onto the same weight, and the ranking stops discriminating between candidate decisions (Figure~\ref{fig:gaweak}). Up to $n=50$ it matches \texttt{la} exactly, at $n$ choices and zero conflicts; at $n=60$ it already requires $1.67$\,million choices and $136.5$\,s of solving time, before timing out from $n=70$ on. The limit is not the cost of the unrolling: at $n=70$, the first size \texttt{ga} fails to solve, that unrolling is $0.6$\,s of grounding and $36$ megabytes over the lazy variant. It is the weight field, into which the reconstructed value of the aggregate has to fit.

\texttt{ga\_weak} avoids the early search failure of \texttt{ga}, but times out from $n=150$, even though its weight expression is the same as \texttt{ga}'s: the equality binding is satisfied only once every element of the sum is assigned, so the heuristic almost never activates and weight saturation is not the explanation for its lack of guidance. At $n=120$ its measured search effort is higher and less regular than the heuristic-free baseline, taking about $50\%$ more choices and correspondingly longer to solve (Table~\ref{tab:bsp-n120}).

Omitting the negative guards without unrolling the aggregate bounds therefore yields no usable guidance: it increases search effort and slightly reduces the solvable horizon. Read together with \texttt{ga}, the pair delimits the ground side of the design space from both ends: the faithful ground simulation works only while the weights fit, and the cheap approximation does not work at all.

\section{Evaluating Pre-Materialized Auxiliary Predicates}
\label{sec:aux-form}

The \texttt{la\_aux} variant investigates whether the primary advantage of lazy evaluation stems simply from reducing the number of \texttt{\_\_heuristic} directives, or from keeping the evaluation of heuristic conditions entirely outside the ground program. In \texttt{la\_aux}, heuristic conditions and aggregate values are pre-materialized into auxiliary ASP predicates (\texttt{h\_b(X,S)} and \texttt{h\_c(X,S)}), which the lazy propagator then queries during search.

Defining auxiliary rules forces the grounder to instantiate the full Cartesian product of elements and partial sums $(X, S)$. As a consequence, solver variables increase to $132{,}756$ at $n=50$, compared to just $5{,}150$ in direct \texttt{la}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/bsp/expl_laaux_solving.png}\\[6pt]
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{hpc/\ThesisGraphQuality/bsp/expl_laaux_decide_calls.png}
    \caption{Solving time on the C++ backend (top) and number of propagator decide calls on the Prolog backend (bottom) comparing \texttt{la\_aux} against direct \texttt{la} in BSP.}
    \label{fig:laaux}
\end{figure}

The auxiliary materialization costs performance on both backends (Figure~\ref{fig:laaux}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item On C++, maintaining the auxiliary state dominates the cost. At $n=60$, state synchronization and candidate updates take $284.2$\,s of the $288.1$\,s spent solving, while decision callbacks take only $3.4$\,ms in total. The variant times out from $n=70$ onward; direct \texttt{la} keeps solving time negligible.
    \item The auxiliary conditions yield no applicable candidates at decision points on either backend. Search therefore needs many more consultations than direct \texttt{la}: $573$ at $n=20$ and $8{,}452$ at $n=30$, instead of $n$. Prolog benefits most from the cheaper trail updates: synchronization previously consumed $99.2\%$ of the run at $n=30$, its last solved size. With foreign predicates, \texttt{la\_aux} reaches $n=80$, two sizes beyond the native frontier.
\end{itemize}

Reducing the number of heuristic directives is therefore not enough on its own: the benefit comes from keeping the evaluation of the heuristic conditions out of the ground program. Once those conditions are materialized as auxiliary rules, the combinatorial cost simply moves from the directives to the base encoding, and the lazy propagator must maintain the resulting auxiliary state during search.

\section{Effect of the Constraint Formulation}
\label{sec:constr-form}

Every result reported for BSP so far shares one confound: the base program is enormous, and it is the same for all variants. The \texttt{\_co} family removes it. The transformation replaces the quadratic difference check over the two sums with a single constant-bound interval constraint (Section~\ref{sec:bsp}), and it is applied to all six variants of the matrix, so that each has a twin differing from it in the domain encoding alone. Two questions follow, and the family answers them separately: what the reformulation does to the frontier, and, once the base program costs nothing, whether the heuristics still make any difference.

In the standard formulations (\texttt{gc}, \texttt{la}, and \texttt{lc}), this quadratic constraint dominates the grounding phase: at $n=120$ it produces about $52.8$\,million propositional rules and requires between two and three minutes of grounding. Direct lazy evaluation (\texttt{la}) removes the heuristic directives, but the ground program remains bound by the size of the domain specification.

    \caption{Grounding time (top) and propositional rule count (bottom) for BSP on the C++ backend, comparing the compact constraint formulation (\texttt{la\_co}) against the baseline encodings (\texttt{gc}, \texttt{la}, and \texttt{lc}).}
    \label{fig:laco}

\end{figure}

The reformulation changes formula size and grounding time by orders of magnitude (Figure~\ref{fig:laco}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{gc}, \texttt{la}, and \texttt{lc} overlap near the top of both plots, sharing the same quadratic base encoding, whereas \texttt{la\_co} flattens along the horizontal axis. At $n=120$ the propositional formula drops from $52.8$\,million rules to $366$.
    \item Grounding all but disappears. Total clingo runtime at $n=120$ falls from $154.2$\,s in \texttt{la} to $6$\,ms, while retaining a short conflict-free search ($117$ choices, compared with $120$ in the standard encoding). The same holds up to $n=200$, which is solved in $32$\,ms of clingo time; the corresponding \texttt{runlim} wall time of $0.15$\,s reported in Table~\ref{tab:alpha-frontier} additionally includes process startup, and the two figures are not in conflict.
\end{itemize}

The two bottlenecks are therefore independent. Once lazy heuristics remove the search cost, what remains in BSP is dominated by the materialization of the domain constraints, and pairing lazy heuristics with a compact encoding addresses both at once.

\paragraph{On search, the answer is no.} Once the balance constraint is stated compactly, BSP stops being a search problem, and Figure~\ref{fig:co-family} shows the two axes parting company: the grounding curves fan out over four orders of magnitude while the choice curves stay bunched together. The heuristic-free \texttt{gc\_noheur\_co} solves $n=200$ in four milliseconds, taking $60$ choices with zero conflicts, because the interval constraint propagates: fixing part of the partition determines the rest without a decision. No variant improves on that. \texttt{la\_co}, which carries the guidance that on the standard encoding reduced search from $78{,}565$ choices to $120$, here takes $197$, more than three times the baseline, since it insists on deciding every element along the greedy path where propagation would have settled most of them for free. The ground variants land in between, at $100$ to $114$. Search costs about a second at worst for any of them, so none of this is a performance difference; it is the observation that on this encoding there is no search left for a heuristic to improve.

This is worth stating plainly because it bounds the BSP results of Sections~\ref{sec:bsp-runtime} and~\ref{sec:clingo-semantics}. The dramatic search separations reported there, three orders of magnitude between \texttt{la} and the baseline, are separations on a formulation whose difficulty is an artifact of the quadratic constraint. They measure the guidance faithfully, but the thing being guided is a search that a better domain encoding does not have.

\paragraph{On representation, the answer is yes, and it is now measured cleanly.} The same table makes the opposite point on the other axis. With the base program at $604$ rules, everything above that number \emph{is} the heuristic, and the spread is the widest in the thesis. At $n=200$, the two lazy variants ground $606$ rules in about twenty milliseconds. The very short runs yield low or zero sampled RSS values, which are insufficient to establish an exact memory bound. The three ground variants materialize between $8.10$ and $8.16$ million rules, spend $26$ to $44$ seconds doing so, and reach $0.8$ to $4.3$ GB. That is a factor of $13{,}500$ in formula size and of three orders of magnitude in grounding time. With the base formulation held constant, the comparison isolates the effect of the heuristic representation; previously, the base program contributed tens of millions of rules to every row alike. Section~\ref{sec:footprint} counted directives; this measures what they cost once nothing else is in the way.

\paragraph{Two secondary findings.} First, \texttt{lc\_co} reproduces \texttt{gc\_noheur\_co} exactly, the same choice counts and zero conflicts at every size ($60$ choices at $n=200$), as \texttt{lc} reproduces \texttt{gc\_noheur} on the standard encoding: the inertness of the clingo reading is a property of the heuristic body, not of the domain encoding it sits in. What the \texttt{\_co} family adds is the price tag. Its ground twin \texttt{gc\_co} buys the identical guidance, none, for $44.3$\,s of grounding, $8.2$ million rules and $4.3$ GB, and ends up taking $100$ choices where the baseline takes $60$. The claim of Section~\ref{sec:clingo-semantics}, that an inert \emph{materialized} heuristic is worse than no heuristic, holds here in its sharpest form.

Second, \texttt{ga\_co} solves the entire range, including the sizes at which \texttt{ga} times out. Its weights saturate clasp's $16$-bit field exactly as \texttt{ga}'s do, since the two encodings differ only in the balance constraint and the weight expression is untouched, but the saturation costs nothing here, because the ranking it corrupts is not what is carrying the run. The limit identified in Section~\ref{sec:weight-problem} is therefore a limit on the ranking rather than on the encoding: it bites exactly when the heuristic is doing the work, which on the standard BSP encoding it is and on the compact one it is not.
