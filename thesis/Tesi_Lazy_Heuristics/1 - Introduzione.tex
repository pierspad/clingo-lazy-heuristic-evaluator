\section{Introduction and Background}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{The Cost of a Dynamic Heuristic}
\label{sec:motivation}

Answer Set Programming (ASP) is a declarative approach to combinatorial search. Instead of prescribing an algorithm step by step, the modeller describes the properties that an admissible solution must satisfy, and a solver enumerates the assignments that satisfy them \cite{gebser2012asp,clingoGuide}. The price of that convenience is control: the modeller says \emph{what} a solution looks like, but not \emph{in which order} the search should look for one.

Domain-specific heuristics are the language in which that order is given back to the modeller. In clingo they are written as declarative annotations that tell the solver which atom to decide next and with which polarity, and on hard industrial instances they can be the difference between a run that finishes in seconds and one that does not finish at all \cite{gebser2013heuristics,dodaro2016combining}. They are, however, part of the program, and clingo instantiates the program before search starts. A heuristic is therefore expanded, in full, before the first decision is taken.

That expansion is harmless as long as the heuristic depends only on the input. It stops being harmless as soon as the heuristic depends on \emph{how the search is going}, which is precisely when a heuristic is most useful. Consider the directive used throughout this thesis to guide Balanced Set Partitioning, the problem of splitting $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ into two sets \mintinline{text}|b/1| and \mintinline{text}|c/1| of nearly equal sum:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
#heuristic b(X) : x(X), not c(X), S = #sum{Y : c(Y)}, W = ((n+1)*S)+X. [W, true]
\end{minted}

The rule reads: prefer to place the element $X$ into $b$, the more strongly the larger the sum $S$ of what is already in $c$. It is a natural greedy policy, and it is dynamic, because $S$ is a property of the partial solution built so far, not of the instance. The aggregate value $S$ appears \emph{inside the weight}, and the atoms \mintinline{text}|c(Y)| it sums over are guessed by the search rather than given as facts. The grounder cannot know what $S$ will be. Its only option is to enumerate every value the sum could reach and to materialise a separate ground directive for every pair $(X,S)$: on this encoding, $\Theta(n^{3})$ objects, more than a million at $n = 100$, none of which carries any information the modeller wrote down. A device introduced to make the problem easier is what puts the answer out of reach.

The alternative is to leave the heuristic symbolic and to evaluate it while the search runs, when the partial assignment that $S$ refers to actually exists. Lazy-grounding systems do this for the program as a whole, and Alpha \cite{weinzierl2017blending} does it for heuristics in particular \cite{comploi2023domain,comploi2023dynamicAggregates}; but they do it inside a different solver, with a different architecture and a different maturity. This thesis asks whether the same effect can be obtained inside clingo, without giving up its ground-and-solve pipeline and without touching its solver core: the heuristic stays a handful of ground facts, and a propagator registered with the solver interprets those facts at every decision point, against the assignment as it stands (Figure~\ref{fig:pipelines}). The result is not a lazy grounder for ASP. It is a lazy evaluator for one specific, and specifically expensive, part of the program.

The rest of this section fixes the notions the construction rests on, in the order in which it needs them: the semantics of ASP (Section~\ref{sec:bg-asp}), the ground-and-solve architecture and the search loop the propagator plugs into (Section~\ref{sec:bg-cdcl}), the \mintinline{text}|#heuristic| language and the two conventions of clingo that the thesis keeps returning to (Section~\ref{sec:bg-heuristics}), Alpha's reading of partial assignments, which is the semantic reference for the whole work (Section~\ref{sec:bg-alpha}), and the interfaces through which clingo can be extended at solving time (Section~\ref{sec:bg-propagator}). Section~\ref{sec:contributions} then states the objective and the contributions precisely.

\subsection{Answer Set Programming}
\label{sec:bg-asp}

Answer Set Programming is a declarative programming paradigm rooted in the stable model semantics of logic programming \cite{gelfond1988stable,gebser2012asp}. A \emph{normal rule} has the form
\[
a \;\leftarrow\; b_1, \ldots, b_m,\; \mathit{not}\; c_1, \ldots, \mathit{not}\; c_n,
\]
where $a$, the $b_i$ and the $c_j$ are atoms: the head $a$ is derived whenever every positive body atom $b_i$ is derivable and no negative body atom $c_j$ is. The negation $\mathit{not}$ is \emph{default negation}: $\mathit{not}\; c$ holds in the absence of a derivation for $c$, not because $c$ has been proven false. This is what makes the semantics non-monotonic, and it is why the meaning of a program cannot be given by fixpoint iteration alone.

It is given instead through the notion of a stable model. Fix a set of atoms $X$, the candidate solution, and a ground program $P$. The \emph{reduct} $P^X$ is obtained by deleting every rule whose negative body intersects $X$ --- those rules are the ones $X$ itself invalidates --- and by deleting the negative literals from the rules that survive, which $X$ has already satisfied. The reduct is a positive program, so it has a unique least model, and $X$ is a \emph{stable model} (or \emph{answer set}) of $P$ exactly when it coincides with that least model \cite{gelfond1988stable}. The definition is a consistency test: $X$ is stable when, assuming what $X$ assumes to be false, one derives precisely $X$. On the two-rule program $\{\,a \leftarrow \mathit{not}\; b\,;\; b \leftarrow \mathit{not}\; a\,\}$ the candidate $X = \{a\}$ gives the reduct $\{a \leftarrow\}$, whose least model is $\{a\}$: stable. The candidate $\{a,b\}$ deletes both rules, leaving the least model $\emptyset$: not stable. Intuitively, then, a stable model is a set of atoms both closed under the rules and justified by them, in which every atom has a non-circular derivation that survives the model's own assumptions about what is false.

Problem solving in ASP follows the \emph{guess-and-check} idiom: a choice construct opens the solution space, integrity constraints (rules with an empty head) prune the candidates that violate the specification, and the answer sets of the program correspond one-to-one to the solutions of the problem. The input language of modern systems extends normal rules with first-order variables, arithmetic, choice rules such as \mintinline{text}|{ b(X) } :- x(X)|, conditional literals, and aggregate atoms such as \mintinline{text}|#sum|, \mintinline{text}|#count|, \mintinline{text}|#min| and \mintinline{text}|#max|, which condense a set of weighted contributions into a single comparable value \cite{clingoGuide}. Aggregates are central to what follows: the heuristics under study rank decisions by expressions such as \enquote{the current sum of the elements already placed in a set}, and how and \emph{when} such an aggregate is evaluated is exactly what separates the systems compared here.

\subsection{Ground-and-Solve: gringo, clasp, and Conflict-Driven Search}
\label{sec:bg-cdcl}

clingo \cite{gebser2019multishot} couples the grounder gringo with the solver clasp into a single system. The grounder instantiates the first-order program over its Herbrand universe, producing a variable-free program; \emph{intelligent instantiation} restricts this expansion to rule instances whose positive body is potentially derivable, so the ground program is in general much smaller than the full instantiation, but it must still be materialised in full before search starts. The solver clasp \cite{gebser2007clasp} then computes the stable models of the ground program with \emph{conflict-driven nogood learning} (CDNL), the ASP counterpart of the CDCL algorithm that underlies modern SAT solvers \cite{moskewicz2001chaff}. The solver maintains a \emph{partial assignment}, a consistent set of literals over the program atoms; it alternates deterministic \emph{unit propagation} with nondeterministic \emph{decisions}, and when propagation derives a contradiction it analyses the conflict, learns a nogood that prevents its repetition, and \emph{backjumps} to an earlier decision level. Which atom to decide on, and with which polarity, is delegated to a \emph{decision heuristic}; clasp's default is an activity-based scheme in the VSIDS family \cite{moskewicz2001chaff}, which favours atoms that appear in recent conflicts.

Two features of this architecture matter for the thesis. First, every atom and every rule the solver will ever see must exist before the first decision is made: whatever the modeller wants the heuristic to consult at runtime has to be compiled, at grounding time, into propositional material. Second, the size of the ground program is the dominant memory cost of the pipeline, and when a program fragment multiplies value domains, its instantiation grows polynomially or worse in the instance parameters. This \emph{grounding bottleneck} is a well-known limitation of ground-and-solve systems \cite{comploi2023domain}; Section~\ref{sec:results} measures one concrete manifestation of it, namely heuristic directives whose weight depends on an aggregate value.

\subsection{Domain-Specific Heuristics in clingo}
\label{sec:bg-heuristics}

clasp exposes its decision heuristic to the modeller through the \mintinline{text}|Domain| heuristic and the \mintinline{text}|#heuristic| directive \cite{gebser2013heuristics}:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
#heuristic A : Body. [W@P, M]
\end{minted}

For every ground instance whose $Body$ holds, the directive attaches to the atom $A$ a \emph{modification} with weight $W$, priority $P$ and modifier $M$. The modifiers refine different aspects of the decision: \mintinline{text}|sign| fixes the polarity with which $A$ is decided, \mintinline{text}|level| places $A$ in a ranking consulted before the default heuristic, \mintinline{text}|true| and \mintinline{text}|false| combine a level with a sign, and \mintinline{text}|init| and \mintinline{text}|factor| perturb the scores of the underlying activity-based heuristic rather than overriding it. Runs must select \mintinline{text}|--heuristic=Domain| for any of this to take effect.

Two conventions of this language are worth stating explicitly here, because the whole design of the thesis is organised around them. The first concerns \emph{ranking}. The priority \mintinline{text}|@P| does \emph{not} rank different atoms against each other: it arbitrates between multiple modifications targeting the \emph{same} atom, the highest priority winning, while the global order in which distinct atoms are decided is induced by the resolved level value, that is, by the weight alone. The second concerns \emph{truth}. The $Body$ of a ground directive is evaluated with respect to \emph{established} truth: a condition \mintinline{text}|not a| contributes only once $a$ has been assigned false, and an aggregate has a value only once its source atoms are determined. Both conventions are natural in a pipeline that grounds first and searches afterwards, and both differ from the lazy-grounding reading described next. That pair of differences is what generates the experimental design of Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

\subsection{Lazy Grounding, Alpha, and Heuristics over Partial Assignments}
\label{sec:bg-alpha}

Lazy grounding attacks the grounding bottleneck at its root, by interleaving grounding and solving: rule instances are produced during search, when their positive bodies become satisfied by the current assignment, instead of being materialised up front. Alpha \cite{weinzierl2017blending} is the reference system of this family; it combines lazy grounding with a CDNL-style search loop, so it retains conflict learning while never holding the full ground program in memory.

Alpha's role in this thesis, however, is not that of a competing solver but that of a \emph{semantic} reference. Comploi-Taupe et al.\ extended Alpha with declaratively specified domain-specific heuristics evaluated against the \emph{partial} assignment maintained during solving \cite{comploi2023domain}. Their semantics differs from clingo's on exactly the two points listed above. On truth, a negative heuristic condition $\mathit{not}\; a$ is satisfied as soon as $a$ is \emph{not assigned true}, so a heuristic fires on undecided atoms instead of waiting for established falsity. On ranking, the annotations $(W,P)$ form \emph{global levels}: every applicable heuristic with priority $P$ strictly precedes every heuristic with a lower priority, regardless of weights, and the weight discriminates only inside a level. A subsequent extension added \emph{dynamic aggregates} to the heuristic language \cite{comploi2023dynamicAggregates}: aggregate expressions in heuristic conditions are re-evaluated over the atoms currently true in the partial assignment, so their value evolves as search proceeds. This is what makes a heuristic such as \enquote{prefer the element whose insertion keeps the two sums balanced} directly expressible, and the two papers demonstrate the approach on configuration benchmarks, including the Partner Units Problem \cite{aschinger2011pup} and the House Reconfiguration Problem \cite{friedrich2011reconfiguration} used here.

The gap this thesis addresses is now visible. clingo offers a mature and efficient ground-and-solve pipeline whose heuristic conditions read established truth and whose aggregates are frozen at grounding time. Alpha offers heuristics that read the partial assignment and re-evaluate aggregates dynamically, but inside a different, lazily grounded system. Reproducing the second reading inside the first architecture, without paying the ground materialisation that a faithful ground simulation would require, is the subject of Sections~\ref{sec:methodology} and~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Extending clingo: Propagators and the Heuristic Interface}
\label{sec:bg-propagator}

Nothing in the previous sections suggests that clingo can be made to evaluate anything at search time: the pipeline grounds, then searches, and the searching half only ever sees propositional material. clingo does, however, expose a supported way in: \emph{propagators}, user-supplied components registered with the solver that take part in the search loop \cite{gebser2019multishot}. The mechanism exists so that theories the ASP language cannot express natively --- difference constraints, linear arithmetic, scheduling propagators --- can be attached to clasp as external reasoners without forking it. This thesis uses the same door for a different purpose: not to add inference, but to add \emph{guidance} that is computed rather than materialised.

A propagator is defined by four callbacks. During \mintinline{text}|init| it inspects the symbolic atoms of the ground program, maps the atoms it cares about to solver literals, and registers \emph{watches} on them. During search, \mintinline{text}|propagate| is invoked when a watched literal becomes true, \mintinline{text}|undo| when backtracking retracts it, and \mintinline{text}|check| on total assignments. The watches are what make the mechanism incremental and therefore affordable: the propagator is woken only on the trail changes it declared interesting, and can maintain its own state in lockstep with the solver's partial assignment rather than recomputing it.

The \mintinline{text}|Clingo::Heuristic| interface extends this contract with a \mintinline{text}|decide| callback, invoked at each decision point \emph{before} the solver's built-in heuristic: it receives the current assignment and either returns a signed literal, which becomes the next decision, or returns $0$, in which case clasp falls back to its default heuristic. Taken together, the two interfaces give exactly what the construction sketched in Section~\ref{sec:motivation} needs, and nothing more: watches to observe the partial assignment as it evolves, and \mintinline{text}|decide| to act on what has been observed. The lazy heuristic mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:methodology} is a heuristic propagator in this sense --- it keeps the declarative heuristic descriptions in memory, evaluates them incrementally against the trail, and answers each \mintinline{text}|decide| call with the best applicable target --- and it is built entirely against the public API, without modifying clasp.

\subsection{Objective and Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The objective of this thesis is to design, implement and evaluate an extension of clingo that evaluates domain-specific heuristics lazily, and to compare it systematically against the ground-and-solve alternative. Because Section~\ref{sec:bg-heuristics} and Section~\ref{sec:bg-alpha} isolated two independent differences between the two systems --- \emph{where} the heuristic is evaluated and \emph{how} its conditions read partial information --- the comparison is organised as a two-by-two design over those two axes, developed in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

Three questions structure the work.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Representation.} How can a dynamic heuristic be expressed in a compact ground form that a propagator can interpret, and can the clingo-like and Alpha-like readings of partial information coexist in a single mechanism, selected per rule?
    \item \textbf{Faithfulness.} How much of clingo's heuristic semantics --- weights, priorities, modifiers --- survives a reimplementation on top of the \mintinline{text}|Clingo::Heuristic| interface, and what does the mismatch between clingo's and Alpha's reading of priorities cost in encoding effort?
    \item \textbf{Payoff.} What does the lazy representation actually buy, in grounding size, memory and search behaviour, on problems whose heuristics are dynamic in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}?
\end{enumerate}

The implemented system consists of two modified clingo builds sharing the same source layout. The build in \path{clingo-native} carries the native backend: shared types, a parser for \mintinline{text}|__heuristic| facts, incremental aggregate states and the heuristic propagator, all under \path{libclingo}. The build in \path{clingo-prolog} carries the Prolog backend: the same propagator skeleton, a rule-string normaliser, an in-process SWI-Prolog evaluation backend \cite{wielemaker2012swipl} and an auxiliary clingo-based fallback evaluator. In both builds the propagator is registered automatically in \path{clingo_app.cc}, so the modified binaries use the mechanism without any external controller. The two backends embody two different engineering answers to the same question --- compiled incremental evaluation against a general logic-programming runtime, over the same declarative content --- and comparing them is part of the evaluation.

On the experimental side, the project provides encodings of three benchmark problems --- Balanced Set Partitioning (BSP), the Partner Units Problem (PUP) \cite{aschinger2011pup} and the House Reconfiguration Problem (HRP) \cite{friedrich2011reconfiguration} --- in every cell of the design and for both backends, under \path{test_folder/encodings-native} and \path{test_folder/encodings-prolog}; an equivalence harness, \path{tools/check_equivalence.py}, which verifies that the heuristics preserve the answer sets and that the Prolog backend is actually active; and a benchmark pipeline built on \mintinline{text}|benchmark-tool| and \mintinline{text}|runlim| \cite{runlim}, with wrapper systems for the two binaries, a result parser that extracts solver and propagator statistics, and automatic graph generation.
