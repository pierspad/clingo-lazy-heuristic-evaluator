\section{Introduction and Background}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{The Cost of a Dynamic Heuristic}
\label{sec:motivation}

Answer Set Programming (ASP) is a declarative approach to combinatorial search. Instead of describing an algorithm step by step, the modeller describes the properties that an admissible solution must satisfy, and a solver enumerates the assignments that satisfy them \cite{gelfond1988stable, gebser2012asp, clingoGuide}.
There are typically two types of approaches, ground-and-solve and lazy grounding.
In ground-and-solve systems, such as clingo, the relevant part of the program is instantiated before search starts, and this includes the domain-specific heuristics that tell the solver where to look first. The approach is strongly memory bound, and the limit shows up in two distinct ways: as the instances grow, and whenever a domain-specific heuristic depends on the state of the computation --- for instance through an aggregate over the atoms that are true \emph{at that point of the search}. The grounder cannot know that state, so its only option is to enumerate every value the state could take, and a single heuristic ends up costing a number of ground objects that is combinatorial in the size of the instance.
In lazy-grounding systems the two phases are interleaved, which extends the reach on memory-intensive problems at the cost of a slower search.

An ASP solver, put bluntly, is an engine highly specialised in backtracking, effective on NP-hard problems and driven by an unusually elegant domain-specific language.
The cost of having this elegant and convenient DSL is control: the modeller defines \emph{what} solution he wants, but not \emph{how} the engine should look for it.
A pure backtracking approach behaves as an uninformed search, and on problems whose structure is known by construction, guiding the solver with that knowledge is extremely helpful.
\emph{Domain-specific heuristics} are the means by which the modeller feeds that knowledge into the search.
They are not part of the ASP-Core-2 standard: they are a solver-level extension, introduced in clasp as the \mintinline{text}|#heuristic| directive and supported by other systems as well. Syntactically they are declarative annotations that the grounder instantiates like ordinary rules, and their purpose is to tell the solver which atom to decide next, and with which polarity; their impact on solving is well documented \cite{gebser2013heuristics,dodaro2016combining}. They are, however, part of the program, and clingo instantiates the program before search starts. A heuristic is therefore expanded, in full, before the first decision is taken.
That expansion can be relatively harmless as long as the heuristic depends only on the input. It stops being harmless as soon as the heuristic depends on \emph{how the search is going}, which is precisely when a heuristic is most useful. Consider the directive used throughout this thesis to guide Balanced Set Partitioning, the problem of splitting $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ into two sets \mintinline{text}|b/1| and \mintinline{text}|c/1| of nearly equal sum:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
#heuristic b(X) : x(X), not c(X), S = #sum{Y : c(Y)}, W = ((n+1)*S)+X. [W, true]
\end{minted}

The rule reads: prefer to place the element $X$ into $b$, the more strongly the larger the sum $S$ of what is already in $c$.\\
It is a natural greedy policy, and it is \emph{dynamic} in the sense of \cite{comploi2023dynamicAggregates}, because $S$ is a property of the partial solution built so far, not of the instance. The aggregate value $S$ appears \emph{inside the weight}, and the atoms \mintinline{text}|c(Y)| it sums over are guessed by the search rather than given as facts. The grounder cannot know what $S$ will be. Its only option is to enumerate every value the sum could reach and to materialise a separate ground directive for every pair $(X,S)$: on this encoding, $\Theta(n^{3})$ objects, more than a million at $n = 100$, none of which carries any information the modeller wrote down. 
A domain-specific heuristic introduced to help the engine solve a problem quicker ends up putting the answer out of reach.

The alternative is to leave the heuristic symbolic and to evaluate it while the search runs, when the partial assignment that $S$ refers to actually exists. In lazy-grounding solvers this comes natural and it has already been tested \cite{weinzierl2017blending,comploi2023domain,comploi2023dynamicAggregates}; this work explores whether the same effect can be obtained inside clingo, without giving up its ground-and-solve pipeline de facto obtaining a ground-and-solve engine with lazily evaluated heuristics (Figure~\ref{fig:pipelines}) 

The rest of this section fixes the notions this work rests on: the semantics of ASP (Section~\ref{sec:bg-asp}), the ground-and-solve architecture and the search loop the propagator plugs into (Section~\ref{sec:bg-cdcl}), the \mintinline{text}|#heuristic| syntaxes and the two conventions of clingo that the thesis keeps returning to (Section~\ref{sec:bg-heuristics}), Alpha's reading of partial assignments, which is the semantic reference for the whole work (Section~\ref{sec:bg-alpha}), and the interfaces through which clingo can be extended at solving time (Section~\ref{sec:bg-propagator}). Section~\ref{sec:contributions} then states the objective and the contributions precisely.

\subsection{Answer Set Programming}
\label{sec:bg-asp}

A \emph{normal rule} in ASP has the form
\[
a \;\leftarrow\; b_1, \ldots, b_m,\; \mathit{not}\; c_1, \ldots, \mathit{not}\; c_n,
\]
where $a$, the $b_i$ and the $c_j$ are atoms: the head $a$ is derived whenever every positive body atom $b_i$ is derivable and no negative body atom $c_j$ is. The negation $\mathit{not}$ is \emph{default negation}: $\mathit{not}\; c$ holds in the absence of a derivation for $c$. 
The meaning of a program is given instead through the notion of a stable model. Fix a set of atoms $X$, the candidate solution, and a ground program $P$. The \emph{reduct} $P^X$ is obtained by deleting every rule whose negative body intersects $X$, and by deleting the negative literals from the rules that survive. The reduct is a positive program, so it has a unique least model, and $X$ is a \emph{stable model} (or \emph{answer set}) of $P$ exactly when it coincides with that least model \cite{gelfond1988stable}.
On the two-rule program
\[
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
a &\leftarrow \mathit{not}\; b\\
b &\leftarrow \mathit{not}\; a
\end{aligned}
\right\}
\]
the candidate $X = \{a\}$ gives the reduct $\{a \leftarrow\}$, whose least model is $\{a\}$: stable.
The candidate $\{a,b\}$ deletes both rules, leaving the least model $\emptyset$: not stable. 
A stable model is then a set of atoms both closed under the rules and justified by them, in which every atom has a non-circular derivation that survives the model's own assumptions about what is false.

In the \emph{guess-and-check} idiom a choice construct opens the solution space, integrity constraints prune the candidates that violate the specification, and the answer sets of the program correspond one-to-one to the solutions of the problem. ASP syntax extends normal rules with first-order variables, arithmetic, choice rules such as \mintinline{text}|{ b(X) } :- x(X)|, conditional literals, and aggregate atoms such as \mintinline{text}|#sum|, \mintinline{text}|#count|, \mintinline{text}|#min| and \mintinline{text}|#max|, which condense a set of weighted contributions into a single comparable value \cite{clingoGuide}.
Aggregates are central to this work: the heuristics under study rank decisions by expressions such as \enquote{the current sum of the elements already placed in a set}, and how and \emph{when} such an aggregate is evaluated is exactly what differentiates the systems compared here.

\subsection{Ground-and-Solve: gringo, clasp, and Conflict-Driven Search}
\label{sec:bg-cdcl}

clingo \cite{gebser2019multishot} couples the grounder gringo with the solver clasp into a single system. The grounder instantiates the first-order program over its Herbrand universe, producing a variable-free program; gringo restricts this expansion to rule instances whose positive body is potentially derivable, so the ground program is in general much smaller than the full instantiation, but it must still be materialised in full before search starts. The solver clasp \cite{gebser2007clasp} then computes the stable models of the ground program with \emph{conflict-driven nogood learning} (CDNL). The solver maintains a \emph{partial assignment}, a consistent set of literals over the program atoms; it alternates deterministic \emph{unit propagation} with nondeterministic \emph{decisions}, and when propagation derives a contradiction it analyses the conflict, learns a nogood that prevents its repetition, and \emph{backjumps} to the latest \emph{first unique implication point}. Which atom to decide on, and with which polarity, is delegated to a \emph{decision heuristic}; clasp's default is an activity-based one in the VSIDS family \cite{moskewicz2001chaff}, which favours atoms that appear in recent conflicts.

This approach entails that every atom and every rule the solver will ever see must exist before the first decision is made and also that the size of the ground program is the dominant memory cost of the pipeline; when a program fragment ranges over the product of several value domains, its instantiation grows polynomially, or worse, in the instance parameters. \emph{Grounding bottleneck} is a well-known limitation of ground-and-solve systems \cite{comploi2023domain}; Section~\ref{sec:results} measures one concrete manifestation of it, namely heuristic directives whose weight depends on an aggregate value.

\subsection{Domain-Specific Heuristics in clingo}
\label{sec:bg-heuristics}

clasp exposes its decision heuristic through the \mintinline{text}|Domain| heuristic and the \mintinline{text}|#heuristic| directive \cite{gebser2013heuristics}:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
#heuristic A : Body. [W@P, M]
\end{minted}

For every ground instance whose $Body$ holds, the directive attaches to the atom $A$ a \emph{modification} with weight $W$, priority $P$ and modifier $M$. Runs must select \mintinline{text}|--heuristic=Domain| for any of this to take effect.
Two notes on this are worth stating. The priority \mintinline{text}|@P| (in clingo) does \emph{not} rank different atoms against each other: it only arbitrates between several modifications attached to the \emph{same} atom, the one with the highest priority winning, while the global order in which distinct atoms are decided is induced by the weight alone. The second concerns the \emph{truth} of the $Body$ of a ground directive which is evaluated with respect to an \emph{established} truth; a condition \mintinline{text}|not a| contributes only once $a$ has been assigned false, and an aggregate has a value only once its source atoms are determined. Both conventions are natural in a pipeline that grounds first and searches afterwards, and both differ from the lazy-grounding reading described next. That pair of differences is what generates the experimental design of Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

\subsection{Lazy Grounding, Alpha, and Heuristics over Partial Assignments}
\label{sec:bg-alpha}

Lazy grounding attacks the grounding bottleneck at its root, by interleaving grounding and solving: rule instances are produced during search, when their positive bodies become satisfied by the current assignment, instead of being materialised up front. Alpha \cite{weinzierl2017blending} is the reference system of this family.

Comploi-Taupe et al.\ extended Alpha with declaratively specified domain-specific heuristics evaluated against the \emph{partial} assignment maintained during solving \cite{comploi2023domain}. Their semantics differs from clingo's on exactly the two points listed above. On truth, a negative heuristic condition $\mathit{not}\; a$ is satisfied as soon as $a$ is \emph{not assigned true}, so a heuristic fires on undecided atoms instead of waiting for established falsity. On ranking, the annotations $(W,P)$ form \emph{global levels}: every applicable heuristic with priority $P$ strictly precedes every heuristic with a lower priority, regardless of weights, and the weight discriminates only inside a level. A subsequent extension added \emph{dynamic aggregates} to the heuristic language \cite{comploi2023dynamicAggregates}: aggregate expressions in heuristic conditions are re-evaluated over the atoms currently true in the partial assignment, so their value evolves as search proceeds. This is what makes a heuristic such as \enquote{prefer the element whose insertion keeps the two sums balanced} directly expressible, and the two papers demonstrate the approach on configuration benchmarks, including the Partner Units Problem \cite{aschinger2011pup} and the House Reconfiguration Problem \cite{friedrich2011reconfiguration} used here.

\subsection{Extending clingo: Propagators and the Heuristic Interface}
\label{sec:bg-propagator}

Nothing in the previous sections suggests that clingo can be made to evaluate anything at search time, however, it exposes a supported way in: \emph{propagators}, user-supplied components registered with the solver that take part in the search loop \cite{gebser2019multishot}. The mechanism exists so that theories can be attached to clasp as external reasoners without modifying solver's code. This thesis uses the same door for a different purpose: not to add inference, but to add \emph{guidance} that is computed rather than materialised.

A propagator is defined by four callbacks. During \mintinline{text}|init| it inspects the symbolic atoms of the ground program, maps the atoms it cares about to solver literals, and registers \emph{watches} on them. During search, \mintinline{text}|propagate| is invoked when a watched literal becomes true, \mintinline{text}|undo| when backtracking retracts it, and \mintinline{text}|check| on total assignments. The watches are what make the mechanism incremental and therefore affordable by getting woken only on the trail changes it started "watching".

The \mintinline{text}|Clingo::Heuristic| interface extends this contract with a \mintinline{text}|decide| callback, invoked at each decision step \emph{before} the solver's built-in heuristic: it receives the current assignment and either returns a signed literal, which becomes the next decision, or returns $0$, in which case clasp falls back to its default heuristic. Taken together, the two interfaces give exactly what the construction sketched in Section~\ref{sec:motivation} needs: watches to observe the partial assignment as it evolves, and \mintinline{text}|decide| to act on what has been observed. The lazy heuristic mechanism of Sections~\ref{sec:methodology} and~\ref{sec:implementation} is a lazily heuristic evaluator in this sense, it keeps the declarative heuristic descriptions in memory, evaluates them incrementally against the trail, and answers each \mintinline{text}|decide| call with the best applicable target, and it is built entirely against the public \mintinline{text}|Clingo::Propagator| and \mintinline{text}|Clingo::Heuristic| interfaces, without modifying clasp.
