\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{The Experimental Design: Two Axes, Four Variants}

Every experiment in this thesis compares encodings of the same problem that differ \emph{only} in how the domain-specific heuristic is represented and interpreted. The domain logic is held fixed; what varies are the two properties that Section~\ref{sec:bg-heuristics} and Section~\ref{sec:bg-alpha} identified as the points on which clingo and Alpha disagree, and they vary independently.

The \emph{approach} axis fixes where the heuristic is evaluated. In the ground-and-solve approach the heuristic is written with native \mintinline{text}|#heuristic| directives: gringo expands them before search and clasp's Domain heuristic consumes the resulting ground objects. In the lazy approach the heuristic remains a handful of ground \mintinline{text}|__heuristic("..")| facts, which the propagator reads at its initialisation and evaluates at every decision step against the partial assignment.

The \emph{semantics} axis fixes how a heuristic condition reads partial information: the clingo-like semantics, under which \mintinline{text}|not a| holds only once $a$ has been assigned false and an aggregate has a value only once its sources are determined, against the Alpha-like reading, under which \mintinline{text}|not a| holds as soon as $a$ is not assigned true, and an aggregate is computed over the atoms currently true. Only the second reading makes a heuristic dynamic in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}: an instruction such as \enquote{prefer the element whose insertion keeps the sums balanced} presupposes that the sum can be read off an assignment that is still being built, and under the clingo reading that sum simply has no value until it is too late for the guidance to matter.

Crossing the two axes yields the four variants that the rest of the thesis refers to by: \mintinline{text}|gc| and \mintinline{text}|ga| for the ground approach under the clingo and the Alpha semantics, \mintinline{text}|lc| and \mintinline{text}|la| for the lazy evaluated one, the prefix naming the approach and the suffix the semantics. To them is added the heuristic-free baseline \mintinline{text}|gc_noheur|, which carries the same domain logic with no heuristic at all and measures what the solver does when it is left alone (Figure~\ref{fig:axes}).

\subsubsection{Simulating Alpha in Ground clingo}
\label{sec:ga-simulation}

The \mintinline{text}|ga| encodings apply two systematic transformations to \mintinline{text}|gc|.

The first transformation drops the negative literals that test the partial state (for BSP, \mintinline{text}|not c(X)| and \mintinline{text}|not b(X)|; for PUP, the \mintinline{text}|not not_assigned_...| conditions; for HRP, guards such as \mintinline{text}|not fullCabinet(C)|). Under the Alpha reading these literals are satisfied on undecided atoms as well as on false ones, and an undecided atom is the common case at the moment a heuristic fires; removing the literal altogether is the closest approximation, and it is harmless in practice because the solver never re-decides an atom that is already assigned.

The second transformation replaces the equality condition with a \emph{lower/upper} bound. For BSP:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
sum_value(0..tot).
#heuristic b(X) : x(X), sum_value(S), S <= #sum{Y : c(Y)},
    W = ((n+1)*S)+X. [W, true]
\end{minted}

Instead of binding $S$ to the final value of the sum, the directive is unrolled over all candidate values \mintinline{text}|sum_value(S)| and guarded by the condition $S \le \#\mathtt{sum}\{Y : c(Y)\}$. During search, as soon as the partial sum reaches a bound $S$, every directive with that bound becomes applicable; among the applicable ground directives for the same atom, clasp's Domain heuristic retains the one with the maximum weight, which corresponds exactly to the \emph{current} value of the aggregate. The max-weight selection thus reconstructs the dynamic aggregate at solving time. For aggregates whose contribution to the weight decreases rather than increases (such as the free-capacity terms $N_0,N_1,N_2$ in PUP) the same transformation is applied with the bound $V \ge \#\mathtt{max}\{\ldots\}$, so that the max-weight selection picks the smallest proven value.

This simulation is faithful, but its price is exactly the combinatorial explosion that motivated the thesis: one ground directive per point of the product of the unrolled domains, measured in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsubsection{Weights, Priorities, and Flattening}
\label{sec:flattening}

Section~\ref{sec:bg-heuristics} and Section~\ref{sec:bg-alpha} recorded that the two systems read the annotation $[W@P]$ of a heuristic differently. In Alpha the pair is an \emph{absolute} rank over all heuristic instances, read lexicographically: the priority $P$ is a global level and decides first, so no instance of level $P$ is considered while an instance of a level $P' > P$ is still applicable, and the weight $W$ only orders the instances that share the same level. In clingo the two fields do not compose in this way: \mintinline{text}|@P| is local, arbitrating only between the modifications attached to the \emph{same} atom, while the order in which distinct atoms are decided comes from the weight alone. An encoding written for Alpha therefore cannot be handed to clingo verbatim

The intended order can nevertheless be recovered, by \emph{flattening} the level into the weight:
\[
W_{\mathit{flat}} \;=\; W + P \cdot M,
\qquad M > \max W + 1,
\]
where $M$ is a level multiplier strictly larger than any intra-level weight, so that no heuristic of a lower level can outrank a heuristic of a higher one. Each benchmark fixes its own $M$. In the PUP encodings $M$ is the constant offset $100{,}000$ added to the zone weights, against a maximum sensor weight of $46{,}220$ on the benchmark instances; in HRP $M$ is derived from the instance as \mintinline{text}|levelMul(LM) :- LM = MC + MR + 10| over the maximum cabinet and room identifiers. BSP needs no multiplier at all: Section~\ref{sec:bsp} returns to the point.

If $M$ exceeds every intra-level weight, then $W + P\cdot M > W' + P'\cdot M$ holds exactly when $(P,W)$ precedes $(P',W')$, so the total order induced on the atoms is the one Alpha's absolute levels induce.

The suite therefore adopts clingo's convention in \emph{all four variants and in both backends}: absolute levels always live in the weight, and the priority field is left to its local role of arbitrating between several directives attached to the \emph{same} atom. A last convention concerns empty aggregates: \mintinline{text}|#max| over an empty set is $0$ in Alpha but $\#\mathit{inf}$ in clingo. All encodings therefore write \mintinline{text}|#max{0; ...}| with an explicit neutral element, and both lazy backends implement the same rule (the native \mintinline{text}|MaxState| returns $0$ when empty; the Prolog runtime defines \mintinline{text}|dyn_max| with a $0$ default).

\subsection{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The project maintains two modified copies of clingo with the same source layout. The build in \path{clingo-native} implements the native backend; the build in \path{clingo-prolog} implements the Prolog backend and is configured with \mintinline{text}|CLINGO_USE_SWIPL=ON| so that the SWI-Prolog engine is linked in-process and it is easier to account for its impact on memory. In both builds the extension lives entirely under \path{libclingo} and is registered in \path{clingo_app.cc}:

\begin{minted}{cpp}
HeuristicPropagator my_propagator;
ctl.register_propagator(my_propagator, true);
\end{minted}

The propagator is registered on \emph{every} run of the modified binary: on a program that contains no heuristic facts it stays inert, because \mintinline{text}|init| finds nothing to watch and \mintinline{text}|decide| always returns $0$, and the same binary therefore runs the ground variants and the heuristic-free baseline without a separate build. The second argument, \mintinline{text}|true|, asks clasp to call the propagator sequentially, to avoid race conditions on the incremental state described in Section~\ref{sec:runtime}; Nothing else in clingo is touched. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the architecture of the two binary build variants.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{architecture}
\caption{System architecture of the two binary variants (\texttt{clingo-native} and \texttt{clingo-prolog}), showing their respective heuristic propagators and evaluation backends attached to clasp.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The class implements \mintinline{text}|Clingo::Heuristic|, that is, the propagator interface extended with the \mintinline{text}|decide| callback of Section~\ref{sec:bg-propagator}. During \mintinline{text}|init| they scan the symbolic atoms for their trigger facts, \mintinline{text}|__heuristic(...)| in the native build, \mintinline{text}|heuristic("...")| in the Prolog build, build their runtime representation, and register watches on the solver literals they need to observe. During search, \mintinline{text}|propagate| and \mintinline{text}|undo| keep the internal state synchronised with the trail. At each decision point, \mintinline{text}|decide| either returns a signed literal for the best applicable heuristic target or returns $0$, leaving the decision to clasp's fallback heuristic. Runs are launched with \mintinline{text}|--heuristic=Domain|.

The propagator never adds a constraint, never derives a literal and never refuses one. It only reorders the decisions clasp would have taken anyway, so the answer sets are untouched, and a heuristic can make a run slower or faster but cannot change the meaning of the encoding. This is expanded in Section~\ref{sec:validation}.

\subsection{Translation of the Heuristics directives}
\label{sec:dsl}

The native backend does not read \texttt{\#heuristics} facts: the language replaces an entire family of ground directives with a single ground facts that \emph{describes} them. 
This subsection presents what one may write and what each construct means by deriving that fact for the BSP heuristic of Section~\ref{sec:motivation}, one component at a time, leaving to Section~\ref{sec:implementation} the question of how it is executed. 

The starting point is the directive as clingo reads it:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
#heuristic b(X) : x(X), not c(X), S = #sum{Y : c(Y)}, W = ((n+1)*S)+X. [W, true]
\end{minted}

The \textbf{target} atom of the heuristic is \mintinline{text}|b(X)|, where $X$ ranges over the ground instances. The lazy fact names only the \emph{predicate}, \mintinline{text}|__target(b)|; the instances are found by the propagator at \mintinline{text}|init|, when the ground program is available and the enumeration of \mintinline{text}|b(...)| comes for free.\\

The condition \mintinline{text}|x(X)| is part of the \textbf{positive body} and shares its argument with the target: the candidate for \mintinline{text}|b(7)| is alive only while \mintinline{text}|x(7)| is true. Such conditions are written as the bare predicate name, \mintinline{text}|x|, and the propagator matches the position of their arguments against the target tuple.\\

The condition \mintinline{text}|not c(X)| is part of the \textbf{negative body}, likewise matches the position of its arguments with the target tuple, and is written \mintinline{text}|__n_c|, the prefix marking negation.\\

The \textbf{semantics} used to interpret that negation is encoded in the \mintinline{text}|__semantics(S)| selector, that decides whether it is read as \enquote{\mintinline{text}|c(X)| is assigned false} or as \enquote{\mintinline{text}|c(X)| is not assigned true}.\\ 

The \textbf{aggregate} $S = \#\mathtt{sum}\{Y : c(Y)\}$ is the aggregate we want evaluated lazily. We denote it as \mintinline{text}|__sum(c)| and then we bind that value to the variable \mintinline{text}|s| with: \mintinline{text}|__bind(s, __sum(c))|.\\

The \textbf{weight} $((n+1)\cdot S)+X$ cannot be carried over as it stands. A lazy fact is an ordinary ASP term, and gringo evaluates arithmetic inside terms while grounding: an infix expression written there would either be computed once, before search, or --- as is the case here, since $S$ and $X$ are not bound anywhere in the fact --- be rejected as unsafe. The weight is therefore kept \emph{unevaluated}, by writing it in prefix form as a term tree, \mintinline{text}|__weight(__add(__mul(s, B), self))|, which is to say $s \cdot B + \mathit{self}$. The three constructors \mintinline{text}|__add|, \mintinline{text}|__sub| and \mintinline{text}|__mul| are binary, denote the corresponding integer operation, and are the entire arithmetic of the language; their arguments are integer constants, variables bound elsewhere in the same fact (here \mintinline{text}|s|, bound to the sum, and \mintinline{text}|B|, an ordinary ASP variable that grounding replaces by $n+1$), or the symbol \mintinline{text}|self|, which denotes the first argument of the ground target --- the $X$ of \mintinline{text}|b(X)|. Arguments of the target other than the first are reached with \mintinline{text}|__bind_target_arg|, described below.\\

The parser turns that tree into a small arithmetic AST once, at \mintinline{text}|init|. From then on it is evaluated \emph{one candidate at a time}: at every decision, each candidate that is currently active evaluates its own copy of the expression, with \mintinline{text}|self| holding that candidate's target argument and \mintinline{text}|s| holding whatever the sum is at that instant of the search. One expression therefore stands for as many numbers as there are targets, multiplied by the values the aggregate takes over the course of the search, and none of those numbers is ever materialised in the ground program.\\

\paragraph{Priority, modifier and semantics.} The three remaining fields carry the annotations of the original directive.

\mintinline{text}|__priority(0)| carries the priority in the local role fixed by Section~\ref{sec:flattening}. It is $0$ here because \mintinline{text}|b/1| is the target of a single directive, and it is $0$ in every BSP and PUP encoding for the same reason; the field does real work only in HRP, where the value $1$ separates the reuse and filling directives from the closing \mintinline{text}|false| directives that share their targets.

\mintinline{text}|__modifier(true)| carries clingo's modifier unchanged, with the same admissible values: \mintinline{text}|level| sets the ranking value alone, \mintinline{text}|sign| the preferred polarity alone, and \mintinline{text}|true| and \mintinline{text}|false| set both, the first preferring the target true and the second preferring it false. The field is not decoration, because \mintinline{text}|decide| returns a \emph{signed} literal and the modifier is what fixes that sign. Every heuristic in the suite uses \mintinline{text}|true| except the closing level of HRP, which uses \mintinline{text}|false| to drive towards false the choice atoms that no other directive claims; omitting the modifier there would fall back to the default \mintinline{text}|true| and invert the level, turning the pruning of the search tail into an instruction to populate it.

\mintinline{text}|__semantics(alpha)| selects the reading of negation and aggregates, and is precisely the field that distinguishes an \mintinline{text}|la| encoding from its \mintinline{text}|lc| twin: between the two, nothing else in the file changes. Assembling the components gives the fact in full:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
__heuristic(__target(b), x, __n_c, __bind(s, __sum(c)),
            __weight(__add(__mul(s, B), self)), __priority(0),
            __semantics(alpha), __modifier(true)) :- B = n + 1.
\end{minted}

The body \mintinline{text}|:- B = n + 1| is only a helper: gringo evaluates arithmetic inside terms, so \mintinline{text}|__mul(s, n+1)| written directly in the head would already reduce to \mintinline{text}|__mul(s,101)| for $n = 100$, and the two forms are interchangeable here. What the body does is decouple the template from the numbers it needs, and it earns its keep as soon as those numbers are not program constants but values computed from the instance. HRP is the case in point: its level multiplier is derived by the encoding itself,

\begin{minted}{prolog}
maxCabinetId(M) :- M = #max{ C : cabinetDomain(C) }.
maxRoomId(M)    :- M = #max{ R : roomDomain(R) }.
levelMul(LM)    :- LM = MC + MR + 10, maxCabinetId(MC), maxRoomId(MR).

__heuristic(__target(reuseCabinet), legacyCabinet,
            __weight(__add(6, __mul(4, LM))), __priority(1),
            __semantics(alpha), __modifier(true)) :- levelMul(LM).
\end{minted}

so the same heuristic file adapts to every instance without being edited, and without the propagator ever having to know what a program constant, an instance or an aggregate over a domain is: by the time it reads the fact, \mintinline{text}|LM| is a number. The body is the seam at which the whole of ASP remains available for computing the constants of a heuristic, while the fact itself stays a plain template. \subsubsection{Joins, Target Arguments and Filtered Aggregates}
\label{sec:dsl-joins}

BSP's heuristic is the easy case: every one of its conditions ranges over the target's own tuple, so a bare predicate name says everything. Two constructs cover what the other benchmarks need.

\paragraph{Explicit joins.} When a positive body predicate does \emph{not} share the target tuple --- because it has a different arity, because its arguments sit in different positions, or because a value has to be read out of it and used in the weight --- the correspondence is stated explicitly:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
__body(pred, __match(src, tgt), __bind_arg(var, idx))
\end{minted}

\mintinline{text}|__match(src, tgt)| requires argument \mintinline{text}|src| of the body atom to be equal to argument \mintinline{text}|tgt| of the target (all indices are 0-based, and at least one \mintinline{text}|__match| is mandatory, since a body atom unrelated to the target would say nothing about the candidate); \mintinline{text}|__bind_arg(var, idx)| extracts argument \mintinline{text}|idx| of the body atom into a variable usable in the weight and priority expressions. A minimal instance makes the mechanics visible. Given the facts \mintinline{text}|score(1,10)|, \mintinline{text}|score(2,5)|, \mintinline{text}|score(3,7)| and the targets \mintinline{text}|choose(1)|, \mintinline{text}|choose(2)|, \mintinline{text}|choose(3)|, the fact

\begin{minted}{prolog}
__heuristic(__target(choose),
            __body(score, __match(0,0), __bind_arg(w,1)),
            __weight(w), __modifier(true)).
\end{minted}

pairs each \mintinline{text}|choose(X)| with the \mintinline{text}|score(X,W)| that agrees with it on the first argument, binds \mintinline{text}|w| to the second argument of that atom, and so gives \mintinline{text}|choose(1)| weight $10$, \mintinline{text}|choose(2)| weight $5$ and \mintinline{text}|choose(3)| weight $7$: the propagator decides \mintinline{text}|choose(1)| first. Written without \mintinline{text}|__body|, the same heuristic would have to enumerate one directive per score value, which is the ground behaviour the lazy encoding exists to avoid. \mintinline{text}|__bind_target_arg(var, idx)| plays the dual role on the target itself, and is how a target of arity greater than one is read beyond its first argument, which \mintinline{text}|self| already covers. Together these constructs turn a template into a join: when several body atoms match one target, each combination becomes a candidate of its own, which is why candidate materialisation (Section~\ref{sec:runtime}) is not simply one candidate per ground target.

\paragraph{Filtered aggregates.} An aggregate, likewise, need not range over the whole extension of its source predicate: it can be restricted \emph{per target}. PUP needs, for each zone, the maximum number of sensors already placed on the \emph{previous} communication unit, and writes it

\begin{minted}{prolog}
__bind(m1, __max(num_sensors_on_unit, 0, __filter(1,1,-1)))
\end{minted}

which is read from the inside out. The source predicate is \mintinline{text}|num_sensors_on_unit|, whose first argument is a sensor count $N$ and whose second is a unit $U'$. The \mintinline{text}|0| that follows the predicate name is the index of the \emph{aggregated} argument, so the value being maximised is $N$; it is a position, not a default. The \mintinline{text}|__filter(1,1,-1)| is the restriction, with the general form \mintinline{text}|__filter(src_idx, tgt_idx, offset)|: it keeps only the source atoms whose argument $1$ equals argument $1$ of the target plus the offset, here $U' = U - 1$. For the target \mintinline{text}|assigned_zone_unit(Z,U)| the whole binding therefore means: \emph{let \mintinline{text}|m1| be the largest sensor count recorded, among the atoms currently true, for the unit immediately preceding this target's unit}. The offset may be omitted and defaults to $0$ --- the sibling bindings \mintinline{text}|m0| and \mintinline{text}|m2| of the same rule use $0$ and $+1$ for the current and the following unit --- and several filters may be given, in which case they are conjoined; the empty-set convention of Section~\ref{sec:flattening} gives the value $0$ when no source atom survives them.

Two things are worth noticing about this binding. First, the value it denotes is different for every target, and changes as the search proceeds, yet the fact that declares it is a single line whose size is independent of the instance: this is the same economy as the weight expression, applied to the data the weight reads. Second, a filter is a linear relation between one source argument and one target argument, which is a deliberate limitation rather than an oversight --- it is exactly what the aggregate states of Section~\ref{sec:runtime} can maintain incrementally, one update per source atom that changes, without ever re-scanning the source predicate at a decision.

One restriction is worth declaring: the aggregate machinery draws from a single source predicate, so joins occurring inside an Alpha-style aggregate cannot be expressed directly. Where the original encodings need one, it is precompiled into an auxiliary derived predicate --- for PUP, \mintinline{text}|sensor_assigned_zone/2| and \mintinline{text}|sensor_zone_on_unit/3| --- which the aggregate then reads as a single source. These auxiliaries are pure definitions that appear in no constraint, so they cannot change the answer sets; they do add grounding, and that cost is charged to every variant alike, so it cannot flatter the lazy ones.

This closes the design: the two axes, the conventions on weights and priorities that make the four variants comparable, the place the propagator occupies inside clingo, and the language in which a heuristic is handed to it. What remains is the machinery that executes those descriptions --- the parser, the runtime structures, the incremental aggregates and the two evaluation backends --- together with the benchmarks and the checks the measurements rest on; Section~\ref{sec:implementation} takes them in that order.
