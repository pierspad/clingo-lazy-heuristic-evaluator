\chapter{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\section{The Experimental Design: Two Axes, Four Variants}
\label{sec:experimental-design}

Every experiment in this thesis compares encodings of the same problem that differ in how the domain-specific heuristic is represented and interpreted. The rules that define the problem, its guess and its constraints, are held fixed across the variants of a family; what varies are the two properties that Section~\ref{sec:bg-heuristics} and Section~\ref{sec:bg-alpha} identified as the points on which clingo and Alpha disagree, and they vary independently.

The \emph{approach} axis fixes where the heuristic is evaluated. In the ground-and-solve approach the heuristic is written with native \texttt{\#heuristic} directives: gringo expands them before search and clasp's Domain heuristic consumes the resulting ground objects. In the lazy approach the heuristic remains a handful of ground facts, \texttt{\_\_heuristic(...)} for the native backend and \mintinline{text}|heuristic("...")| for the Prolog one, which the propagator reads at its initialization and evaluates at every decision step against the partial assignment.

The \emph{semantics} axis fixes how a heuristic condition reads partial information: the clingo-like semantics, under which \mintinline{text}|not a| holds only once $a$ has been assigned false and an aggregate has a value only once its sources are determined, against the Alpha-like semantics, under which \mintinline{text}|not a| holds as soon as $a$ is not assigned true, and an aggregate is computed over the atoms currently true. Only the second reading makes a heuristic dynamic in the sense of reacting to a partial assignment during search, as motivated in Section~\ref{sec:motivation}: an instruction such as \enquote{prefer the element whose insertion keeps the two sums balanced} presupposes that the sum can be evaluated against an assignment that is still being built; under clingo's semantics, that sum simply has no value until it is too late for the guidance to matter.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{axes}
\caption{The experimental matrix. Prefixes name the approach, suffixes the semantics.}
\label{fig:axes}
\end{figure}

Crossing the two axes yields the four variants that the rest of the thesis refers to as: \texttt{gc} and \texttt{ga} for the ground approach under the clingo and the Alpha semantics, \texttt{lc} and \texttt{la} for the lazily evaluated one, the prefix naming the approach and the suffix the semantics. To them is added the heuristic-free baseline \texttt{gc\_noheur}, which carries the same domain logic with no heuristic at all and measures what the solver does when it is left alone (Figure~\ref{fig:axes}).

To isolate specific architectural and modeling mechanisms beyond this primary matrix, the experimental design also incorporates two exploratory variants, \texttt{ga\_weak} and \texttt{la\_aux}, and a second copy of the entire BSP matrix, the \texttt{\_co} family, in which the domain constraint is formulated in a different way. Their empirical motivation and methodological role in disentangling the contributions of default negation, body materialization, and constraint formulation are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:exploratory-methodology}.

\subsection{Simulating Alpha semantics}
\label{sec:ga-simulation}

Reproducing an Alpha-like behavior within clingo requires addressing two distinct aspects: default negation over unassigned literals and dynamic aggregate evaluation.

\subsubsection{Default negation over unassigned literals}

The first transformation concerns negative literals in heuristic bodies: \mintinline{text}|not c(X)| in BSP, \mintinline{text}|not not_assigned_sensor_unit(S,U)| in PUP, and the guards \mintinline{text}|not fullCabinet(C)| and \mintinline{text}|not assignedThing(T)| in HRP. 

Clasp applies heuristic modifications only to variables that are currently unassigned, as enforced in \texttt{DomainHeuristic::propagate}:
\begin{minted}{cpp}
if (s.value(a.var) == value_free && a.prio >= prio) { applyAction(s, a, prio); }
\end{minted}
It follows that \emph{a directive whose body entails the truth value of its own target atom can never fire}: once the body conditions hold, the target is already decided, and clasp skips it.

All heuristic directives in the suite that test the search state through negative literals show the same problem:
\begin{itemize}
    \item In \textbf{BSP}, \mintinline{text}|b(X)| and \mintinline{text}|c(X)| are complementary choices, so the condition \mintinline{text}|not b(X)| is satisfied only once the target \mintinline{text}|c(X)| is already derived.
    \item In \textbf{PUP}, the heuristic body repeats the negative literal of the rule that derives the target atom, namely \mintinline{text}|not not_assigned_sensor_unit(S,U)|.
    \item In \textbf{HRP}, the placement directives for \mintinline{text}|cabinetTOthing(C,T)| require the guard \mintinline{text}|not assignedThing(T)|; in ground clingo this holds only when the candidate assignments for $T$ are already proven false, meaning the target is never unassigned (\texttt{value\_free}) when the directive evaluates.
\end{itemize}
Consequently, none of these directives ever fires in the \texttt{gc} variant. In HRP the same encoding also carries directives with no condition at all, which are applied at initialization and do steer the search; the conditional ones listed above stay inert.

The ground variant \texttt{ga} therefore drops these negative literals from the heuristic bodies. 
For directives where the negated literal simply tests the target atom or its complement (as in BSP and PUP), this simplification is completely safe: nothing is lost that could have fired, and clasp's \mintinline{text}|value_free| check still prevents modifying an already assigned atom. 
In HRP, however, the dropped literals also include capacity guards such as \mintinline{text}|not fullCabinet(C)|, which describe a shared resource rather than the target atom itself. There, dropping the guard is a necessary static approximation: it enables the directive to fire on unassigned atoms from the start of search, at the price of never deactivating once a cabinet becomes full.

Deleting \mintinline{text}|not a| from a heuristic body preserves the Alpha reading exactly when $a$ is the target atom or its complement, and only then: in that case the deleted guard duplicates a test clasp performs anyway, since a directive is applied only to a \texttt{value\_free} variable. When $a$ is any other atom, the guard carries information the solver does not re-derive, and removing it changes which decisions the heuristic proposes. 


\subsubsection{Dynamic aggregates by bound-guarded unrolling}

The second transformation replaces dynamic aggregate equality with bound-guarded unrolling over all candidate values. For BSP:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
sum_value(0..tot).
#heuristic b(X) : x(X), sum_value(S), S <= #sum{Y : c(Y)},
    W = ((n+1)*S)+X. [W, true]
\end{minted}

Here the directive is unrolled over all \texttt{sum\_value(S)} and guarded by a bound condition, so that during search the currently satisfied bounds encode the current aggregate value, provided the guard is monotone, i.e.\ once satisfied it stays satisfied as further atoms become true.

Without explicit priorities all unrolled directives share the default priority $0$. Clasp applies newly satisfied directives by overwriting previous ones; it therefore retains the \emph{most recently activated} directive, not the maximum-weight one. To match the reference heuristic, the unrolling must align the activation order with the weight order:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lower-bound guards ($V \le f$):} As $f$ increases, the active set $\{V : V \le f_{\mathit{curr}}\}$ expands upwards. The last activated value is $V = f_{\mathit{curr}}$, which yields the maximum weight if $W(V)$ is strictly \emph{increasing} (e.g.\ in BSP, where $W(S) = (n{+}1)S + X$).
    \item \textbf{Upper-bound guards ($V \ge f$):} The active set $\{V : V \ge f_{\mathit{curr}}\}$ instead expands downwards, as $f$ decreases, and the alignment requires $W(V)$ to be strictly \emph{decreasing}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Methodological Rationale for the Exploratory Variants}
\label{sec:exploratory-methodology}

While the four main variants evaluate the core matrix of approach versus semantics, the following are introduced as experimental controls to isolate specific factors:

\paragraph{Isolating Alpha-Negation from Dynamic Aggregates (\texttt{ga\_weak}).}
Alpha-like semantics treat default negation as satisfied over unassigned literals, and evaluate aggregates dynamically over currently true atoms. While \texttt{ga} simulates both via extensive ground unrolling, \texttt{ga\_weak} acts as an ablation study that applies Alpha's negation semantics while avoiding unrolling aggregates. This investigates whether adapting default negation alone provides any improvement during search, or whether dynamic aggregate evaluation is strictly indispensable for effective search guidance.

\paragraph{Fewer directives, or less grounding? (\texttt{la\_aux}).}
The lazy variants differ from the ground ones in two ways at once: the grounder emits far fewer heuristic-related objects, and the evaluation of the heuristic conditions stays out of the ground program altogether. The \texttt{la\_aux} variant separates the two effects. It keeps the lazy propagator, but writes the conditions of the BSP heuristic as ordinary ASP rules, one atom \texttt{h\_b(X,S)} for each element $X$ and each candidate sum $S$, which the propagator then queries at decision time. The heuristic directives stay few, while the pre-evaluation goes back to the grounding phase; the variant therefore shows which of the two is responsible for the gain.

\paragraph{Disentangling Heuristic and Problem Constraint Bottlenecks (the \texttt{\_co} family).}
In the baseline BSP encoding, the balance requirement is enforced by computing the sums of both partitions independently and verifying that their difference does not exceed one via an integrity constraint (\texttt{:- sumB(SB), sumC(SC), (SB - SC)**2 > 1}). Because the possible partition sums scale quadratically with $n$, joining all pairs of candidate sums during grounding triggers a severe combinatorial explosion (materializing over $52.8$\,million propositional rules at $n=120$). Consequently, total runtime remains entirely dominated by grounding the base problem constraints. This imposes an artificial performance floor across all variants, masking the true computational footprint of the heuristic layer.

The compact (\texttt{\_co}) transformation resolves this bottleneck by exploiting the invariant that the total sum of all elements is known a priori ($tot = \sum_{i=1}^n i$). Instead of generating auxiliary predicates for both sums and checking them quadratically, the balance condition is restated as a single aggregate constraint with constant interval bounds, requiring the sum of partition $b$ to fall strictly within $[\lfloor tot / 2 \rfloor, \lceil tot / 2 \rceil]$ (Section~\ref{sec:bsp}). This removes the cross-product join over candidate sums entirely, collapsing the base encoding by five orders of magnitude (from millions of rules to just a few hundred). As an experimental control, the \texttt{\_co} family eliminates the base grounding overhead, cleanly isolating the representational cost of the heuristics and clarifying whether dynamic guidance remains beneficial when domain propagation is already efficient.

\subsection{Weights, Priorities, and Flattening}
\label{sec:flattening}

Section~\ref{sec:bg-alpha} recorded that the two systems read the annotation $[W@P]$ of a heuristic differently. In Alpha, the pair is an \emph{absolute} rank over all heuristic instances, read lexicographically, with $P$ compared first: the priority $P$ is a global level and decides first, so no instance of level $P$ is considered while an instance of a level $P' > P$ is still applicable, and the weight $W$ only orders the instances that share the same level. In clingo, the evaluation is different: $P$ is local, arbitrating only between heuristics relative to the \emph{same} target atom, while the order in which distinct atoms are decided comes from the weight alone. An encoding written for Alpha therefore cannot be handed to clingo verbatim.

The intended order can nevertheless be recovered, by \emph{flattening} the level into the weight:
\[
W_{\mathit{flat}} \;=\; W + P \cdot M,
\qquad M > \max W - \min W,
\]
where the intra-level weights $W$ range over $[\min W, \max W]$ and the level multiplier $M$ is larger than that range. Under this condition $W + P\cdot M > W' + P'\cdot M$ holds whenever $P > P'$, regardless of the two individual weights, and reduces to $W > W'$ when $P = P'$: the total order induced on the atoms is then the one Alpha's absolute levels induce. When all weights are non-negative, which is the case in every encoding of this suite, $M > \max W$ suffices.

Each benchmark fixes its own $M$. In the PUP encodings $M$ is the constant offset $100{,}000$ added to the zone weights, against a maximum sensor weight of $26{,}220$ on the benchmark instances; in HRP $M$ is derived from the instance as \mintinline{text}|levelMul(LM) :- LM = MC + MR + 10| over the maximum cabinet and room identifiers, which gives $16$ on the smallest instance and $54$ on the largest. BSP does not need a multiplier, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:bsp}.

\subsection{Problems on Bigger Weights}
\label{sec:weight-problem}
clasp stores the weight of a \texttt{\#heuristic} modification in a signed 16-bit integer field and clamps any value exceeding $32{,}767$. Consequently, two ground directives whose weights both exceed $32{,}767$ become indistinguishable to clasp's Domain heuristic. A flattened weight must therefore satisfy $W + P\cdot M \le 32{,}767$ for the ground variants to rank as intended. 
HRP stays far below this bound; PUP exceeds it on the zone directives, with the consequences discussed in Section~\ref{sec:pup}; and BSP, whose maximum weight grows as $\Theta(n^{3})$, crosses it within the benchmarked range. What matters there is not the largest weight the grounder emits, but the largest one the search actually reaches: directives whose aggregate value is never attained may clamp without any observable effect. On BSP that effective crossing occurs at $n=51$, as analyzed in detail in Section~\ref{sec:ga-weak}. 

In the compact \texttt{\_co} encoding (Section~\ref{sec:co-family}), weights saturate in the exact same way, yet runtime is unaffected because propagation resolves choices directly without needing heuristic guidance.
The lazy backends avoid this limitation altogether. Rather than assigning static weights to clasp, the propagator ranks candidate targets dynamically at runtime and directly returns the chosen decision literal.

The suite adopts clingo's convention in \emph{all four variants and in both backends}: absolute levels always live in the weight, simplifying the propagator as it does not need to account for a separate priority field. Once global levels are folded into weights, weight becomes the sole ordering criterion under both semantics. Accordingly, the native lazy language (Section~\ref{sec:dsl}) omits the priority field entirely.

A last convention concerns empty aggregates: \texttt{\#max} over an empty set is $0$ in Alpha but $-\infty$ (\texttt{\#inf}) in clingo. All encodings therefore write \mintinline{text}|#max{0; ...}| with an explicit neutral element, and both lazy backends implement the same rule (the native \texttt{MaxState} returns $0$ when empty; the Prolog runtime defines \texttt{dyn\_max} with a $0$ default).

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The project maintains two modified copies of clingo. They share the same upstream sources and differ only in their extensions: the build in \path{clingo-native} carries the C++ backend, while the build in \path{clingo-prolog} carries the Prolog backend, configured with \texttt{clingo\_USE\_SWIPL=ON} so that the SWI-Prolog engine is linked in-process, making its memory impact easier to account for. In both builds, the extension lives entirely under \path{libclingo}, exposes the same class name, and is registered at the same point of \path{clingo_app.cc}:

\begin{minted}{cpp}
HeuristicPropagator my_propagator;
ctl.register_propagator(my_propagator, true);
\end{minted}

The propagator is registered on \emph{every} run of the modified binary. On a program that contains no lazy heuristic facts, the propagator itself is inactive: \texttt{init} finds nothing to watch and \texttt{decide} always returns $0$, so every decision is left to clasp's own branching heuristic. The same binary therefore also runs the ground variants (with clingo's standard \texttt{\#heuristic}s) and the heuristic-free baseline without requiring a separate build. 

The second argument, \texttt{true}, instructs clasp to call the propagator sequentially, avoiding race conditions on the incremental state described in Section~\ref{sec:runtime}. No other parts of clingo are modified. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the architecture of the two binary build variants.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{architecture}
\caption{System architecture of the two binary variants, showing their respective heuristic propagators and evaluation backends attached to clasp.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

In both builds, the class implements \texttt{clingo::Heuristic}, the propagator interface extended with the \texttt{decide} callback (Section~\ref{sec:bg-propagator}). Both backends follow the same lifecycle, built on \texttt{init}, \texttt{propagate}, \texttt{undo} and \texttt{decide}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=1pt]
    \item During \texttt{init}, they scan symbolic atoms for trigger facts (\texttt{\_\_heuristic(...)} in the C++ build and \mintinline{text}|heuristic("...")| in the Prolog build), construct their runtime representation, and register watches on solver literals.
    \item During forward search, \texttt{propagate} updates the internal state of the aggregates appearing in the heuristic bodies, together with the candidate states, as watched literals become true.
    \item During backtracking, \texttt{undo} restores those aggregate and candidate states as literals are retracted from the trail.
    \item At each decision point, \texttt{decide} either returns a signed literal for the highest-ranked target or returns $0$, delegating the decision to clasp's fallback heuristic.
\end{itemize}
The two backends are not symmetric in what they can express. The native backend maintains an incremental state for \texttt{\#sum}, \texttt{\#count}, \texttt{\#min} and \texttt{\#max} over one predicate and one argument position, and can only filter the aggregated set by matching its arguments with those of the target atom; the Prolog backend evaluates an arbitrary \texttt{aggregate\_all/3} goal and is unrestricted in this respect. Section~\ref{sec:dsl} states the fragment each backend accepts, and Section~\ref{sec:dsl-limits} what falls outside it.

To activate this mechanism, runs are launched with the flag \texttt{--heuristic=Domain}. A detailed discussion of the architectural differences between the two backends is provided in Chapter~\ref{sec:implementation}.

The propagator never adds a constraint, never derives a literal, and never refuses one. It only reorders the decisions clasp would have taken anyway, so the answer sets are untouched, and a heuristic can make a run slower or faster but cannot change the meaning of the encoding (detailed in Section~\ref{sec:decision}).

\section{Translation of the Heuristic Directives}
\label{sec:dsl}

Neither backend reads native \texttt{\#heuristic} directives directly. Instead, a custom declarative language replaces each directive with a single ground fact that \emph{describes} the heuristic rule.

This substitution is driven by two main reasons:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=2pt]
    \item Gringo treats a \texttt{\#heuristic} directive as an ordinary ASP rule and immediately grounds it. By the time the propagator initializes in \texttt{init}, the combinatorial explosion has already occurred. The propagator needs the high-level \emph{template} which gringo does not ground, clingo C++ API does not expose but the propagator can read.
    \item Aggregates must survive grounding unevaluated so they can be monitored dynamically during search. Standard ASP syntax provides no such mechanism: an aggregate inside a rule body is either evaluated statically by the grounder or unrolled over its value domain.
\end{itemize}

The lazy fact is therefore the original directive rewritten in a form that passes through the grounder untouched. Its structure mirrors the original construct-by-construct. 
The remainder of this section details this representation. We introduce each construct by rewriting the BSP heuristic (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}) step by step. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:dsl-limits} outlines the expressive limitations of both backends.

\subsection{Lazy heuristic syntax for the C++ backend}

\begin{minted}{prolog}
#heuristic b(X) : x(X), not c(X), S = #sum{Y : c(Y)}, W = ((n+1)*S)+X. [W, true]
\end{minted}

\paragraph{Target.} The target atom is \texttt{b(X)}, where $X$ ranges over the ground instances. The lazy fact specifies only the predicate name, \texttt{\_\_target(b)}. During \texttt{init}, the propagator queries clingo's atom table for all ground instances of \texttt{b(...)} already materialized by gringo.

\paragraph{Positive body.} The condition \texttt{x(X)} shares its argument with the target: the candidate for \texttt{b(7)} is alive only while \texttt{x(7)} is true. A condition of this shape is written as the bare predicate name, \texttt{x}, and the propagator matches its arguments against the target tuple position by position.

However, a body predicate may have a different arity; its arguments may sit in different positions; or a value may have to be read out of it and used in the weight. The correspondence is then stated explicitly:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
__body(pred, __match(src, tgt), __bind_arg(var, idx))
\end{minted}

\texttt{\_\_match(src, tgt)} requires the argument at the \texttt{src} position of the body atom to equal the argument at the \texttt{tgt} position of the target atom, where both \texttt{src} and \texttt{tgt} are integers. All indices are 0-based, and at least one \texttt{\_\_match} is mandatory. \texttt{\_\_bind\_arg(var, idx)} extracts argument \texttt{idx} of the body atom into a variable.

Given the ground facts:
\begin{minted}{prolog}
score(1,10). score(2,5). score(3,7).
\end{minted}
and the target atoms:
\begin{minted}{prolog}
choose(1). choose(2). choose(3).
\end{minted}
the directive:
\begin{minted}{prolog}
__heuristic(__target(choose), __body(score, __match(0,0), __bind_arg(w,1)), __weight(w), __modifier(true)).
\end{minted}

pairs each \mintinline{text}|choose(X)| with the \mintinline{text}|score(X,W)| that agrees with it on the first argument, and binds \mintinline{text}|w| to the second argument of that atom. The weights are then $10$, $5$ and $7$, so the propagator decides \mintinline{text}|choose(1)| first.

\paragraph{Negative body.} The condition \mintinline{text}|not c(X)| likewise matches its arguments against the target tuple. Negative body literals are specified by prefixing the predicate name with \texttt{\_\_n\_}.

\paragraph{Semantics.} The reading of that negation is fixed by the \texttt{\_\_semantics(S)} selector, which decides whether \texttt{\_\_n\_c} means \enquote{\texttt{c(X)} is assigned false} or \enquote{\texttt{c(X)} is not assigned true}. The same selector fixes the reading of aggregates, described below. It takes \texttt{alpha} or \texttt{clingo}, and defaults to \texttt{clingo}, so that a directive written without it behaves as the native \texttt{\#heuristic} it replaces.

\paragraph{Aggregates.} Aggregates are evaluated lazily during search. For instance, \texttt{\_\_bind(s, \_\_sum(c))} binds variable \mintinline{text}|s| to the sum over the predicate \texttt{c} (by default aggregating its first argument). The operators \texttt{\_\_count}, \texttt{\_\_min}, and \texttt{\_\_max} follow the same structure.

An aggregate can be restricted using \mintinline{text}|__filter(src_idx, tgt_idx, offset)|, which narrows it \emph{per target}, keeping only source atoms whose argument at position \texttt{src\_idx} equals argument \texttt{tgt\_idx} of the target plus an optional \texttt{offset}. For instance, in PUP, retrieving the maximum number of sensors on the \emph{previous} unit ($U-1$) for a target \mintinline{text}|assigned_zone_unit(Z,U)| is expressed as:
\begin{minted}{prolog}
__bind(m1, __max(num_sensors_on_unit, 0, __filter(1, 1, -1)))
\end{minted}
where \mintinline{text}|0| selects the aggregated argument $N$ in \mintinline{text}|num_sensors_on_unit(N, U')|, and the filter matches $U' = U - 1$.

\paragraph{Weight.} Weight is expressed as a \emph{term tree} such as:
\begin{minted}{prolog}
__weight(__add(__mul(s, B), self))
\end{minted}
which represents $(s \cdot B + \mathit{self})$, where \texttt{self} is a shorthand for the target atom's argument at index 0 without requiring a variable binding.

The binary constructors \texttt{\_\_add}, \texttt{\_\_sub}, and \texttt{\_\_mul} form expression trees whose leaves belong to three categories: integer constants, variables of the heuristic language, and target arguments (accessed through \texttt{self} or \texttt{\_\_bind\_target\_arg(var, idx)} for any other).

At \texttt{init}, the propagator compiles all weight term trees into ASTs. During search, each active heuristic object evaluates its weight AST dynamically against the current trail state, avoiding the memory overhead of materializing them in the ground program. Only candidates with a strictly positive resolved weight are taken into account; the others are ignored, leaving their decision to the solver's default heuristic.

\paragraph{Modifier.} The \texttt{\_\_modifier(M)} selector specifies which of clingo's heuristic modifiers to use: \texttt{level} sets the ranking value alone, \texttt{sign} sets the preferred polarity alone, while \texttt{true} (the default) and \texttt{false} set both. 

The solver's \texttt{decide} interface expects a \emph{signed} literal, so a ranking value alone does not determine a decision. Each target keeps one slot per channel, ranking value and preferred polarity, and when several directives write to the same slot the one with the highest weight wins. This is what replaces clingo's local priority. Across the benchmarks all directives use \texttt{true}, except for the final layer of HRP, which uses \texttt{false} to prune the remaining choice atoms.

\medskip
Assembling the components gives the fact in full:

\begin{minted}{prolog}
__heuristic(__target(b), 
            x, 
            __n_c, 
            __bind(s, __sum(c)), 
            __weight(__add(__mul(s, B), self)), 
            __semantics(alpha), 
            __modifier(true)) 
                :- B = n + 1.
\end{minted}


\subsection{Lazy heuristic syntax for the Prolog backend}
\label{sec:prolog-notation}

Unlike the native backend, which only supports a fixed set of predefined constructs, the Prolog backend imposes no syntactic restrictions. A heuristic is declared as a string inside a \mintinline{text}|heuristic("...")| fact and parsed directly as a standard Prolog clause; consequently, its body accepts any goal that the engine can prove.

\begin{minted}{prolog}
heuristic(b(X), W, 0, true) :-
    holds(x(X)),
    target_available(b(X)),
    alpha_not(c(X)),
    aggregate_all(sum(Y), holds(c(Y)), S),
    holds(heuristic_base(Base)),
    W is Base * S + X.
\end{minted}

The rule is structured into two main parts:
the \textbf{head} encodes the four arguments of the \texttt{\#heuristic} directive, the target atom ($A$), the calculated weight ($W$), the priority level ($P$), which is always $0$ because global levels are folded into the weight (Section~\ref{sec:weight-problem}), and the modifier ($M$), while the \textbf{body} specifies the conditions and computations needed to derive them.

To interact with the solver's search state, the backend provides a few dedicated interface predicates: \mintinline{text}|holds/1| succeeds on atoms currently true on the trail and on static ground facts; \mintinline{text}|alpha_not/1| and \mintinline{text}|clingo_not/1| implement the two readings of default negation described in Section~\ref{sec:bg-alpha}; and \mintinline{text}|target_available/1| discards candidates that are already assigned.

Everything else uses standard Prolog: arithmetic relies on \mintinline{text}|is/2|, and set aggregations use \mintinline{text}|aggregate_all/3|. For convenience, the backend provides the \mintinline{text}|dyn_*| wrappers (\mintinline{text}|dyn_sum/3|, \mintinline{text}|dyn_count/2|, \mintinline{text}|dyn_min/3|, and \mintinline{text}|dyn_max/3|); in particular, when no matching atoms are yet assigned on the trail (such as at the beginning of search), \mintinline{text}|dyn_min/3| and \mintinline{text}|dyn_max/3| safely return $0$ instead of failing, allowing the heuristic to compute a weight and guide early decisions.

\subsection{What the Two Languages Cannot Express}
\label{sec:dsl-limits}

While Table~\ref{tab:translation} summarizes the constructs supported by each backend, the two engines do not cover the same subset of clingo's heuristic language, nor does either support standard ASP heuristics in their entirety.

\paragraph{The C++ fragment.} 
The C++ backend implements a restricted subset of \texttt{\#heuristic} directives, constrained along five main dimensions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Target.} Directives specify target \emph{predicates} rather than individual ground atoms. Consequently, every ground instance of that predicate becomes a candidate target and cannot be restricted upfront except through template body conditions. Furthermore, arguments are indexed strictly as numeric tuples, preventing the use of non-numeric symbols.
    \item \textbf{Body.} Every body literal must connect to the target tuple through at least one \texttt{\_\_match}. Crucially, \emph{arbitrary joins between body atoms are not supported} unless they route through the target: a conjunction such as \mintinline{text}|p(X,V), q(V,W)| cannot be expressed directly and must be precomputed via an auxiliary ASP predicate in the encoding. Similarly, relational comparisons between bound variables (e.g., \mintinline{text}|N < M|) are unsupported.
    \item \textbf{Aggregates.} The four aggregate operators (\texttt{\_\_sum}, \texttt{\_\_count}, \texttt{\_\_min}, and \texttt{\_\_max}) operate strictly over a single predicate and a single argument. Filtering is limited to equating an aggregate argument to a target argument via \texttt{\_\_filter}. Consequently, aggregates cannot contain internal joins, conditional conditions, or references to variables bound elsewhere in the rule body.
    \item \textbf{Weights.} Arithmetic expressions are restricted to addition, subtraction, and multiplication over integer constants, bound variables, and target arguments (excluding division, modulo, and comparisons). Moreover, only candidates with a \emph{strictly positive} resolved weight are ranked; directives evaluating to zero are ignored, delegating the choice to clasp's default heuristic.
    \item \textbf{Annotations.} The backend supports the \texttt{level}, \texttt{sign}, \texttt{true}, and \texttt{false} modifiers. The \texttt{init} and \texttt{factor} modifiers are rejected at runtime because they manipulate clasp's internal VSIDS activity scores, which cannot be modified through the propagator's \texttt{decide} callback. Additionally, the native language does not provide an explicit priority field: specifying \texttt{\_\_priority} triggers a parse error, requiring global priority levels to be flattened into weights (Section~\ref{sec:flattening}). As discussed in Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion}, this design choice simplifies the propagator state at the expense of requiring an a priori bound on intra-level weights.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The Prolog fragment.} 
In contrast, the Prolog backend provides substantially greater expressive flexibility. Because rule bodies are evaluated as standard Prolog goals, arbitrary joins, comparisons, nested aggregations (via \texttt{aggregate\_all/3}), and non-numeric terms are supported natively. Furthermore, its head includes an explicit priority argument: directives of the form \texttt{heuristic(T, W, P, M)} are ordered lexicographically by the pair $(P, W)$, directly preserving Alpha's two-dimensional priority scheme without requiring weight flattening.
The principal limitation is that Prolog goals may generate target terms that do not exist in the ground program; the backend must therefore filter out these invalid candidates by querying clasp's atom tables (Section~\ref{sec:decision-prolog}). The remaining trade-off is computational rather than expressive: unrestricted goals are re-evaluated from scratch at each decision point, introducing runtime overhead that is evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:per-decision-cost}.

Consequently, the two backends are not interchangeable implementations of the same language.
