\section{Discussion of the Results}

The results answer the question that motivated this thesis: clingo can evaluate declarative heuristics during search while retaining its ground-and-solve architecture. The propagator keeps the heuristic description compact and computes its guidance from the current partial assignment. The benefit depends on three factors: the cost of grounding the heuristic, the meaning assigned to its conditions, and the amount of work required to evaluate it at each decision.

\subsection{What lazy evaluation saves}

The clearest saving is in the representation of the heuristic. On BSP, the ground directives grow as $\Theta(n^3)$ and exceed one million at $n=100$, whereas the lazy description consists of two template facts. This removes the enumeration of possible aggregate values from the heuristic grounding. It does not make the entire solver state constant in size: the base program still has to be grounded, and the propagator maintains runtime data for its targets and aggregates.

The compact BSP encoding makes this distinction particularly clear. At $n=200$, its heuristic-free baseline solves the instance in four milliseconds with only $60$ choices. Guidance is therefore unnecessary on this formulation. Nevertheless, adding ground heuristic directives requires between $26$ and $44$ seconds of grounding and up to $4.3$\,GB of peak memory. The lazy variants ground in about twenty milliseconds (Table~\ref{tab:co-family}). The cost of representing a heuristic can thus remain substantial even when the heuristic provides no search benefit.

With the original BSP encoding, the situation is different. Alpha-like lazy guidance reduces search to $n$ choices with zero conflicts, but grounding the base constraints remains expensive. The largest solved sizes stay close to those of the unguided baseline, and the runs that determine their exact positions approach the time limit. Here, the main result is a reduction in search effort, rather than a robust increase in the largest solvable instance.

PUP shows both the value of guidance and the saving from lazy representation. The unguided baseline reaches $N=80$, while the ground Alpha simulation, \texttt{ga}, and the lazy Alpha variant, \texttt{la}, solve every published \texttt{double} instance up to $N=200$. On the largest instance, the C++ backend reduces total clingo time from $438.6$\,s to $224.0$\,s and peak memory from $24.4$\,GB to $12.7$\,GB. Both variants reach the end of the available set, so this comparison establishes lower cost at equal measured reach. Larger instances would be needed to determine whether the memory saving also allows \texttt{la} to solve instances beyond the reach of \texttt{ga}.

\subsection{Why the semantics matters}

Compact representation alone does not ensure useful guidance. The heuristic must also interpret partial information in the way intended by the modeller. This is why the experiments separate representation, ground or lazy, from interpretation, clingo-like or Alpha-like.

The benchmark heuristics were written for the Alpha-like reading. Under that reading, a negative condition can hold while its atom is unassigned, and a dynamic aggregate can be computed from the atoms currently true. The same conditions under the clingo-like reading may become available too late to guide the intended decision.

The consequences differ across the families. On BSP and PUP, the lazy clingo-like variant finds no applicable candidates during search. On HRP, some lower-level directives remain applicable while higher-level ones are delayed, so the heuristic guides decisions in an ineffective order. At $N=14$, the C++ propagator \texttt{lc} run makes $66{,}717$ choices, compared with $86$ for \texttt{la}. These observations concern the transfer of these particular heuristics between semantics; they do not establish that one semantics is preferable for every encoding.

The ground variants also expose a separate limitation: clasp stores heuristic weights in a int16 field. Flattening Alpha's global levels into weights preserves their mathematical ordering only when the multiplier is large enough and the resulting values remain representable. On BSP, saturation destroys distinctions needed by \texttt{ga}, which loses its greedy behaviour and times out from $n=70$. The lazy propagator ranks candidates internally and returns a decision literal, avoiding this particular weight-field limit.

This distinction also explains why saturation need not cause a timeout. The compact \texttt{ga\_co} encoding uses the same weights and solves the full suite because propagation already makes search easy. The impact of a damaged ranking depends on how much the encoding relies on that ranking.

\subsection{The runtime cost and the choice of backend}

Lazy evaluation moves work into search. Its cost depends both on how often the solver requests a decision and on how much work the evaluator performs for each request. Effective guidance can reduce the first factor, but a complex rule body can still make the second expensive.

The two backends illustrate this trade-off. The native C++ backend maintains candidates and aggregates incrementally and has a much lower decision cost on the measured instances. The Prolog backend supports a broader language, but may enumerate many intermediate candidates when evaluating relational bodies. On HRP at $N=20$, its propagator accounts for approximately $94\%$ of total runtime even though only about one hundred decisions are needed. On PUP and BSP with effective guidance, its share is much smaller (Section~\ref{sec:per-decision-cost}).

The engineering work also highlighted failures that answer-set checks alone cannot detect. Misinterpreted priority annotations and an incorrectly selected Prolog backend can leave the solver producing valid answers while losing the intended guidance. The suite therefore uses flattened weights consistently and checks backend selection and heuristic activity. Decision counters and candidate statistics complement correctness checks by showing whether the mechanism actually participates in search.

\subsection{What the comparison with Alpha establishes}

The external comparison shows where heuristic laziness helps within clingo and where full lazy grounding retains an advantage. On BSP, guided Alpha solves the largest instance with few decisions and no backtracking while avoiding the large base grounding that dominates clingo's runtime. Keeping only the heuristic lazy cannot remove that remaining cost.

On PUP at $N=200$, the C++ lazy propagator takes $227.1$\,s of external wall time, compared with $50.9$\,s for guided Alpha, a factor of about $4.5$. Their measured peak process memories are similar, approximately $12.7$ and $12.8$\,GB. Ground \texttt{ga} takes $444.8$\,s and $24.4$\,GB on the same external measures. Lazy representation therefore reduces the gap substantially, but Alpha remains faster at this size.

HRP gives a different outcome. Its base program is small, and C++ lazy propagator solves the largest instance in $0.51$\,s, compared with $1.79$\,s for Alpha's native heuristic mode and $15.33$\,s for its query-based mode. The two Alpha modes also differ in backtracking, for reasons not diagnosed in this thesis. The comparison therefore supports a conclusion tied to the family and configuration, rather than a general claim that either architecture is faster.

\section{Limitations}

The implementation is a research prototype with a defined language scope. The C++ backend supports a smaller language subset than the Prolog backend, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:dsl-limits}. The benchmarks use heuristics expressible in both, so their agreement does not establish that every Prolog heuristic can be translated to the C++ propagator language. The propagator is also registered sequentially and has no synchronization layer designed for parallel solving.

The experimental protocol uses one run for each combination of variant, instance and backend. It therefore provides no estimate of run-to-run variance or statistical confidence intervals. Large differences in time are accompanied by structural evidence, such as rule counts and search decisions, but their stability still requires repeated measurements. This is especially relevant to the exact BSP frontier, where the deciding runs finish within $7\%$ of the time limit. The comparison with the earlier Prolog synchronization design likewise spans two campaigns rather than repeated paired measurements.

The benchmark selection places further bounds on the conclusions. PUP contains one instance per size from the \texttt{double} family and ends at $N=200$. HRP remains easy for clasp with the baseline and Alpha-like guidance, so it mainly exposes differences in guidance and evaluation overhead. The original BSP formulation creates a difficulty that largely disappears with a compact balance constraint. Its search results must therefore be interpreted as results for that encoding, rather than evidence that BSP inherently requires a domain heuristic.

On PUP and HRP, the C++ backend uses auxiliary predicates for conditions that Prolog evaluates directly, and candidate ordering can also differ. The backend comparison therefore includes differences in ground representation and search, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:backend-summary}.

Memory measurements are peak RSS for the whole process. They include the embedded Prolog runtime where applicable and the JVM in Alpha, and do not separate grounding memory from search memory. Very short runs can also finish before the sampler records a useful peak. In particular, the close PUP memory figures for Alpha and lazy clingo should be described as similar observations from individual runs.

Finally, parts of the Prolog initialization remain specific to the benchmarks, including a whitelist of static predicates and inference of the constant $n$ from the instance. An auxiliary evaluator that re-grounds programs per decision is retained as a functional reference. General deployment would require a clearer declarative configuration interface and broader validation. The finite sanity checks reported in this thesis support the implementation on the tested cases; they are not an exhaustive validation of all accepted programs.

\section{Implications}

For a modeller, the results suggest examining the domain encoding and the heuristic separately. A compact formulation of the constraints may remove the need for guidance, as on BSP. When a dynamic heuristic is still useful, evaluating it lazily can avoid paying for all possible aggregate values before search begins. The auxiliary-form experiment reinforces this point: materializing the heuristic body in ordinary ASP predicates can reintroduce the same grounding cost even if the directive itself stays compact.

For solver integration, the main contribution is a working use of clingo's public heuristic interface. Declarative descriptions can be evaluated against the partial assignment and translated directly into branching decisions. The C++ and Prolog implementations offer different balances between runtime cost and expressiveness. Choosing between them requires considering the heuristic bodies and the expected number of consultations, as well as the size of the ground program.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The first experimental priority is to repeat measurements and broaden the instance sets. Repeated runs would help assess the stability of timeout boundaries, while multiple instances per size would separate size effects from instance-specific difficulty. Larger PUP \texttt{double} instances would test whether the lower memory use of \texttt{la} eventually produces a larger solvable frontier than \texttt{ga}. A generator should first be validated against the published instances, and time and memory failures should be recorded separately.

The C++ propagator language could then be extended to support joins between body literals, comparisons between bound values, integer division and modulo, and a systematic treatment of non-numeric symbols. The \texttt{doubleV} PUP family from \cite{comploi2023dynamicAggregates} provides a concrete target outside the currently supported subset. Supporting a separate global priority level would also avoid requiring a known bound on intra-level weights for every translation, provided its meaning were made explicit across backends.

For Prolog, the next optimization concerns enumeration. Foreign predicates currently materialize a matching relation before yielding its elements. Iterating over indexed data without copying the relation could reduce the cost of bodies with many joins, particularly on HRP. Its benefit would need to be measured across the families, since reducing synchronization already showed that an optimization can shift cost elsewhere.

\begin{samepage}
Integration could be improved through automatic conversion, weight checks, explicit configuration, statistics reporting, parallel solving, and coordinated tie-breaking. Further benchmarks, including the HRP instances associated with \cite{semmelrock2026evolvingcode}, would test transfer to other problems.
\end{samepage}

\section{Conclusions}

This thesis shows that declarative dynamic heuristics can be evaluated lazily within clingo. A propagator reads a compact heuristic description, evaluates it against the current partial assignment, and supplies the next branching decision. The implementation retains clingo's ground-and-solve pipeline while avoiding the expansion of heuristic aggregate values into large sets of ground directives.

The experiments show why this matters in practice. On PUP, lazy evaluation preserves effective guidance while approximately halving total clingo time and peak memory relative to the ground simulation at the largest published instance. On BSP, the compact encoding demonstrates that representation savings remain valuable even when propagation makes the heuristic unnecessary. Together, these results separate the cost of expressing guidance from the benefit of using it.

They also show that the benefit has conditions. The semantics must match the intended reading of partial information, and evaluating the heuristic must remain affordable during search. The C++ backend achieves low consultation costs through incremental maintenance, while Prolog offers greater expressiveness at a higher and more variable evaluation cost. Neither backend removes the grounding cost of the base problem, which explains the remaining gap with Alpha on BSP and PUP.

The contribution is therefore a practical mechanism for reducing heuristic grounding, supported by experiments that identify both its advantages and its limits. It makes dynamic declarative guidance available inside an established solver architecture and provides a basis for broader language support and evaluation on more varied problems.
